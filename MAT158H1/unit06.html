Derivatives

The derivative as a limit, with every rule proved. In analysis we also care about the fine print: differentiable implies continuous, but not the reverse.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

The derivative captures the instantaneous rate of change — the exact slope "right here" — which an average rate of change cannot.

In the real world

Physics (velocity), economics (marginal cost), and above all machine learning, where training a network is gradient descent — repeatedly following derivatives downhill.

Story & open questions

Newton and Leibniz discovered it independently and feuded over credit for a century; Leibniz's $dy/dx$ notation won. Weierstrass's nowhere-differentiable function showed continuity is strictly weaker than differentiability.

By the end you can
  • Compute derivatives from the limit definition.
  • Prove differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous.
  • Prove the product, quotient, and chain rules.

Definition

Definition

$f'(a)=\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$ when the limit exists.

Theorem

Differentiable at $a$ $\Rightarrow$ continuous at $a$. The converse fails: $|x|$ is continuous but not differentiable at $0$.

The rules, proved

Theorem

$(fg)'=f'g+fg'$ and $(f\circ g)'(x)=f'(g(x))g'(x)$.

Proof idea Product rule
$\dfrac{f(x+h)g(x+h)-f(x)g(x)}{h}=\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}g(x+h) +f(x)\dfrac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}.$
Let $h\to0$, using continuity of $g$: $\to f'g+fg'$. ∎
Common pitfalls

• The difference quotient is a limit, not $0/0$.
• Don't drop the inner factor $g'(x)$ in the chain rule.
• Continuity does not imply differentiability.

Practice

Practice 1

From the definition, find $f'(a)$ for $f(x)=\sqrt x$, $a>0$.

Answer
Rationalize: $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt a}$.
Practice 2

Prove differentiability at $a$ implies continuity at $a$.

Hint
$f(x)-f(a)=\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a} (x-a)\to f'(a)\cdot0=0$.
Practice 3

Show $|x|$ is not differentiable at $0$.

Answer
Left and right difference quotients give $-1$ and $+1$; the limit does not exist.
