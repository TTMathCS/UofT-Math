Transcendental Functions

Exponential, logarithm, and trigonometric functions, and their inverses — defined carefully and differentiated. The clean way to build them is to define $\ln$ as an integral and recover everything else from there.

By the end you can
  • Define $\ln$ and $\exp$ rigorously and prove their basic laws.
  • Differentiate exponential, logarithmic, and trig functions.
  • Differentiate inverse trig functions.
  • Use logarithmic differentiation.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Polynomials cannot capture growth that compounds or motion that repeats. Exponentials, logarithms, and trig functions can — they are called "transcendental" because they escape algebra entirely.

In the real world

$e^x$ models compound interest, radioactive decay, and populations; logarithms give us pH, decibels, the Richter scale, and the "bit" of information; trig functions underlie all signal processing — audio, JPEG images, MRI, and GPS all run on Fourier analysis built from sines and cosines.

Story & open questions

Napier invented logarithms in 1614 to shorten astronomers' brutal hand calculations. Euler named $e$ and tied it all together. Later $e$ and $\pi$ were proved transcendental (1873, 1882), which finally settled the 2,000-year-old puzzle of whether you can "square the circle." You cannot.

How the functions are built

The clean MAT137 approach is structural: define $\ln$ by an integral, prove its laws, define $\exp$ as its inverse, then define $a^x$ using $e^{x\ln a}$. This order matters because it keeps irrational exponents and logarithm laws from becoming circular assumptions.

Dependency chain
First: $\ln x=\int_1^x\dfrac1t\,dt$, so $(\ln x)'=1/x$.
Next: log laws follow by comparing derivatives of functions such as $\ln(ax)-\ln x$.
Then: $\exp$ is the inverse of $\ln$, and inverse differentiation gives $(e^x)'=e^x$.
Finally: for $a>0$, define $a^x=e^{x\ln a}$.

Domain discipline

Most mistakes in this unit are not algebra mistakes; they are domain mistakes. Before differentiating, ask where the expression is defined and where the derivative formula is valid.

  • $\ln x$ requires $x>0$, while $\ln|x|$ is defined for $x\ne0$.
  • $a^x=e^{x\ln a}$ requires $a>0$ for real-valued functions of all real $x$.
  • $\arcsin x$ is defined on $[-1,1]$, but its derivative formula only holds on $(-1,1)$.
  • Logarithmic differentiation requires the quantity inside $\ln$ to be positive, or else an absolute-value version must be justified.

The natural logarithm as an integral

Definition Natural log

For $x>0$, $\displaystyle\ln x=\int_1^x \frac{1}{t}\,dt.$ By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, $\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x=\dfrac1x$.

Theorem Log laws

For $a,b>0$: $\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b$ and $\ln(a/b)=\ln a-\ln b$. For rational $r$, $\ln(a^r)=r\ln a$; after real powers are defined by $a^r=e^{r\ln a}$, the same identity holds for every real $r$.

Proof idea of $\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b$
Fix $a$ and let $g(x)=\ln(ax)$. Then $g'(x)=\dfrac{a}{ax}= \dfrac1x=(\ln x)'$.
So $g(x)-\ln x$ is constant; at $x=1$ it equals $\ln a$. Hence $\ln(ax)=\ln a+\ln x$; set $x=b$. ∎

The exponential function

Definition exp

$\exp=\ln^{-1}$, and $e=\exp(1)$. Then $\dfrac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x$, and for $a>0$, $a^x=e^{x\ln a}$ with $\dfrac{d}{dx}a^x=a^x\ln a$.

The derivative rule follows from the inverse function theorem (Unit 3 / next units): if $y=e^x$ then $\ln y=x$, so $\dfrac{y'}{y}=1$, giving $y'=y=e^x$.

Trigonometric functions

Key limit

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1$ — the fact behind every trig derivative. From it, $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin x=\cos x$ and $\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos x=-\sin x$.

Example

$\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan x=\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}= \dfrac{\cos^2x+\sin^2x}{\cos^2x}=\sec^2x.$

Inverse trigonometric functions

Derivatives

$\dfrac{d}{dx}\arcsin x=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$ for $|x|<1$,   $\dfrac{d}{dx}\arctan x=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}$ for every real $x$.

Example arctan
If $y=\arctan x$ then $\tan y=x$, so $\sec^2y\cdot y'=1$.
$\sec^2y=1+\tan^2y=1+x^2$, hence $y'=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}$. ∎

Logarithmic differentiation

For products, quotients, and variable exponents, take $\ln$ first.

Example

Differentiate $y=x^x$ ($x>0$).

$\ln y=x\ln x$, so $\dfrac{y'}{y}=\ln x+1$.
$y'=x^x(\ln x+1)$.
Common pitfalls

• $\dfrac{d}{dx}a^x\ne x a^{x-1}$. The power rule is for $x^{n}$, not $a^x$.
• Mixing up $(\ln x)'=1/x$ with $(\log_{10}x)'=1/(x\ln 10)$.
• Forgetting domains: $\arcsin x$ needs $|x|\le1$, and its derivative formula requires $|x|<1$.

Practice

Problems adapted from the MAT137 question bank by Alfonso Gracia-Saz, used under CC BY-SA 4.0. Write full solutions before opening the answer.

Practice 1 · Log + chain

Differentiate $f(x)=\ln(\sec x+\tan x)$ where it is defined.

Answer
$f'(x)=\dfrac{\sec x\tan x+\sec^2x}{\sec x+\tan x}=\sec x.$
Practice 2 · Inverse trig

Show $\dfrac{d}{dx}\arcsin x=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$ for $|x|<1$.

Hint
$y=\arcsin x\Rightarrow\sin y=x\Rightarrow\cos y\,y'=1$; $\cos y=\sqrt{1-x^2}$ since $|y|\le\pi/2$.
Practice 3 · Prove a rule

Obtain and prove the formula for $\dfrac{d}{dx}\arctan x$.

Solution
$y=\arctan x\Rightarrow \tan y=x\Rightarrow\sec^2y\,y'=1$, so $y'=\dfrac{1}{\sec^2y}=\dfrac{1}{1+\tan^2y}=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}.$
Practice 4 · Absolute value

Find the derivative of $F(x)=\ln|x|$.

Answer
$F'(x)=\dfrac1x$ for $x\ne0$. (For $x<0$, $\ln|x|=\ln(-x)$ and the chain rule gives $\dfrac{-1}{-x}=\dfrac1x$.)
Practice 5 · Product + chain

Differentiate $f(x)=e^{\sin x+\cos x}\ln x$ for $x>0$.

Answer
$f'(x)= e^{\sin x+\cos x}\!\left[(\cos x-\sin x)\ln x+\dfrac1x\right].$
Practice 6 · Log differentiation

Use logarithmic differentiation on $y=\dfrac{(x+1)^2\sqrt{x-3}}{x^4}$.

Hint
$\ln y=2\ln(x+1)+\tfrac12 \ln(x-3)-4\ln x$; differentiate, then multiply by $y$.
Practice 7 · Variable base

Calculate the derivative of $g(x)=x^{\tan x}$ for $x>0$.

Solution
$\ln g=\tan x\,\ln x$, so $\dfrac{g'}{g}=\sec^2x\,\ln x+\dfrac{\tan x}{x}$ and $g'=x^{\tan x}\!\left(\sec^2x\,\ln x+\dfrac{\tan x}{x}\right).$
Practice 8 · Implicit + log

Assume $x,y>0$. Find $y'$ if $x^y=y^x$.

Solution
Take logs: $y\ln x=x\ln y$. Differentiate: $y'\ln x+\dfrac yx=\ln y+\dfrac{xy'}{y}$, so $y'=\dfrac{\ln y-y/x}{\ln x-x/y}.$

Extension 1 · Log derivative

Starting from $\ln x=\int_1^x 1/t\,dt$, explain why $(\ln x)\prime=1/x$ for $x>0$.

Guide
Apply FTC Part 1 to the continuous integrand $1/t$ on $(0,\infty)$.
Extension 2 · Exponential inverse

Use inverse differentiation to show $(e^x)\prime=e^x$ if $e^x$ is the inverse of $\ln x$.

Guide
If $y=e^x$, then $x=\ln y$. The inverse derivative gives $(e^x)\prime=1/(1/y)=y=e^x$.
Extension 3 · Inverse trig

Derive $(rcsin x)\prime=1/\sqrt{1-x^2}$ for $-1<x<1$.

Guide
Let $y=rcsin x$. Then $x=\sin y$ and $y\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$, so $dy/dx=1/\cos y=1/\sqrt{1-x^2}$.
Extension 4 · Log differentiation

Differentiate $y=x^x$ for $x>0$.

Guide
Take logs: $\ln y=x\ln x$. Then $y\prime/y=\ln x+1$, so $y\prime=x^x(\ln x+1)$.
