Rank–Nullity & Matrices

The accounting identity of linear algebra: dimension in = dimension collapsed + dimension out. Once a basis is fixed, every linear map becomes a matrix.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

The Rank–Nullity theorem ties together how much a map crushes to zero and how much it produces. It is the bookkeeping that explains when systems have solutions and how many.

In the real world

Rank measures the true information content of a data matrix; low-rank structure is the basis of compression, recommender systems, and PCA. Nullity is the space of "free" solution directions.

Story & open questions

Representing a linear map by a matrix in coordinates — and recognizing rank as basis-independent — unified the previously separate arts of solving equations and studying transformations.

By the end you can
  • State and use the Rank–Nullity theorem.
  • Build the matrix of a linear map in given bases.
  • Relate rank to solvability of $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$.

The theorem

Theorem Rank–Nullity

For a linear $T:V\to W$ with $V$ finite-dimensional, $$\dim V=\operatorname{rank}(T)+\operatorname{nullity}(T),$$ where rank $=\dim(\text{range})$ and nullity $=\dim(\text{null space})$.

Matrix of a map

Definition

Fix bases of $V$ and $W$. The columns of the matrix $[T]$ are the coordinate vectors of $T$ applied to the basis of $V$. Then $T(v)$ in coordinates is $[T]\,[v]$.

Example

Differentiation on $P_2$ in basis $1,x,x^2$ has matrix with columns $0,\,(1,0,0),\,(0,2,0)$ — rank $2$, nullity $1$ (the constants).

Common pitfalls

• Rank–nullity counts the dimension of the domain, not the codomain.
• The matrix changes with the basis; rank and nullity do not.
• $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ is solvable iff $\mathbf{b}$ lies in the range (column space).

Practice

Practice 1

A linear map $\mathbb{R}^4\to\mathbb{R}^3$ has rank $3$. Find its nullity.

Answer
$4-3=1$.
Practice 2

If a $3\times3$ matrix has nullity $0$, what is its rank?

Answer
$3$ — full rank, invertible.
Practice 3

Can a map $\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^5$ be onto?

Answer
No — rank $\le2<5$.
