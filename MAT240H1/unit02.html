Subspaces, Span & Independence

The three ideas that organize a vector space from the inside: which subsets are themselves spaces, what a set of vectors generates, and when vectors carry genuinely independent information. Together they set up the notion of a basis in Unit 3.

By the end you can
  • Prove or refute that a subset is a subspace with the three-part test.
  • Describe the span of a set and prove it is the smallest subspace containing it.
  • Decide linear independence from the definition, over any field.
  • Prove the standard facts about dependence and redundant vectors.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Span and independence are how we measure "how much" a set of vectors captures — without redundancy. They lead directly to the idea of a basis.

In the real world

Linear independence is feature redundancy in data science; the span of a dataset is the subspace it can represent. Dimensionality reduction (PCA) is exactly this language.

Story & open questions

These notions crystallized as mathematicians sought the smallest set of "directions" needed to describe a space — the search that produced the modern theory of dimension.

Subspaces

Definition Subspace

$W\subseteq V$ is a subspace if it is itself a vector space under the operations of $V$.

Theorem Subspace test

$W\subseteq V$ is a subspace if and only if: (i) $0\in W$; (ii) $u+v\in W$ whenever $u,v\in W$; (iii) $av\in W$ whenever $a\in F$, $v\in W$.

Proof idea
The eight axioms are equations that already hold in $V$, so they hold automatically for elements of $W$. The only things that can fail are the closures and the presence of $0$ and inverses — and $-v=(-1)v$ comes from closure under scalars. ∎
Examples and a non-example
Subspaces: the solution set of a homogeneous system $Ax=0$; the symmetric matrices inside $M_{n\times n}(F)$; the polynomials with $p(0)=0$.
Non-example: the union of the two axes in $\mathbb{R}^2$. It passes the zero and scalar tests but fails closure under addition: $(1,0)+(0,1)=(1,1)$ lies on neither axis.
Theorem

Any intersection of subspaces of $V$ is a subspace. (Unions usually are not — the example above.)

Span

Definition Span

A linear combination of $v_1,\dots,v_k$ is $a_1v_1+\cdots+a_kv_k$ with $a_i\in F$. The span of a set $S$ is the set of all linear combinations of finitely many vectors of $S$ (and $\operatorname{span}\,\emptyset=\{0\}$).

Theorem

$\operatorname{span}(S)$ is a subspace of $V$, and it is the smallest one containing $S$: any subspace containing $S$ contains $\operatorname{span}(S)$.

Proof idea
Sums and scalar multiples of linear combinations are again linear combinations — that gives the subspace. And a subspace containing $S$ is closed under exactly the operations used to build combinations, so it swallows all of them. ∎

Linear independence

Definition

$v_1,\dots,v_k$ are linearly independent if $a_1v_1+\cdots+a_kv_k=0$ forces $a_1=\cdots=a_k=0$. Otherwise they are dependent: some nontrivial combination gives $0$.

Theorem Dependence means redundancy

Vectors $v_1,\dots,v_k$ (with $k\ge2$) are dependent iff some $v_j$ is a linear combination of the others — and then removing $v_j$ does not change the span.

Proof
If $a_1v_1+\cdots+a_kv_k=0$ with some $a_j\ne0$, divide by $a_j$ (field!) and solve for $v_j$. Conversely, if $v_j=\sum_{i\ne j}c_iv_i$, then $\sum_{i\ne j}c_iv_i+(-1)v_j=0$ is a nontrivial combination. ∎
Example The field matters

Take $u=(1,1,0)$, $v=(0,1,1)$, $w=(1,0,1)$.

Over $\mathbb{R}$: $a u+b v+c w=0$ gives $a+c=0$, $a+b=0$, $b+c=0$; substituting, $2c=0$, so $a=b=c=0$ — independent.
Over $\mathbb{Z}_2$: $u+v+w=(2,2,2)=(0,0,0)$ — dependent! The step "$2c=0\Rightarrow c=0$" fails when $2=0$. Independence is a statement about the scalars as much as the vectors.
How to run an independence check Write $a_1v_1+\cdots+a_kv_k=0$, read off one linear equation per coordinate, and solve the homogeneous system. Exactly the trivial solution ⇒ independent; any free variable ⇒ dependent, and the free variable hands you an explicit dependence.
Common pitfalls

• A subspace must contain the zero vector — check it first.
• Independence means the only combination giving $0$ is trivial; it is not "no two are parallel" (three coplanar vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ can be pairwise non-parallel yet dependent).
• Any set containing the zero vector is dependent: $1\cdot0=0$ nontrivially.
• Adding vectors can only grow the span; removing a dependent vector keeps it unchanged.

Practice

Original problems written for this guide in the style of Friedberg, Insel & Spence exercises. Write a full solution before opening the answer.

Practice 1 · Subspace test

Is $\{p\in P(\mathbb{R}):p(0)=0\}$ a subspace of $P(\mathbb{R})$?

Answer
Yes: the zero polynomial qualifies, and if $p(0)=q(0)=0$ then $(p+q)(0)=0$ and $(ap)(0)=0$.
Practice 2 · Independence

Are $(1,1,0),(0,1,1),(1,0,-1)$ independent in $\mathbb{R}^3$?

Answer
No — the third equals the first minus the second, so the set is dependent.
Practice 3 · Span

What is $\operatorname{span}\{(1,0),(0,1)\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$? And $\operatorname{span}\{(1,2)\}$?

Answer
All of $\mathbb{R}^2$; and the line $\{(t,2t):t\in\mathbb{R}\}$ through the origin.
Practice 4 · Union

Show the union of the $x$-axis and $y$-axis is not a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

Solution
$(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ belong, but their sum $(1,1)$ lies on neither axis — closure under addition fails.
Practice 5 · Intersection

Prove that the intersection of two subspaces of $V$ is a subspace.

Solution
$0$ lies in both. If $u,v$ lie in both, then $u+v$ and $av$ lie in each (both are subspaces), hence in the intersection. The three-part test passes.
Practice 6 · Polynomials

Prove $1,x,x^2$ are independent in $P(\mathbb{R})$.

Solution
If $a+bx+cx^2$ is the zero polynomial, all its coefficients vanish by definition of polynomial equality — so $a=b=c=0$. (Or: evaluate at $x=0$ to get $a=0$, then at $x=\pm1$ to force $b=c=0$.)
Practice 7 · Field dependence

Show $(1,1,0),(0,1,1),(1,0,1)$ are independent over $\mathbb{R}$ but dependent over $\mathbb{Z}_2$.

Solution
Over $\mathbb{R}$ the system $a+c=a+b=b+c=0$ forces $2c=0$, so all vanish. Over $\mathbb{Z}_2$ the sum of the three vectors is $(2,2,2)=(0,0,0)$ — a nontrivial dependence, because $2=0$ there.
Practice 8 · New from old

Suppose $u,v,w$ are independent in a real vector space. Prove $u+v,\ v+w,\ u+w$ are independent.

Solution
If $a(u+v)+b(v+w)+c(u+w)=0$, regroup: $(a+c)u+(a+b)v+(b+c)w=0$. Independence gives $a+c=a+b=b+c=0$; subtracting equations yields $b=c$, then $2b=0$, so $a=b=c=0$. (Over $\mathbb{Z}_2$ this fails — the three sums are dependent there.)
