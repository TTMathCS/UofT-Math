Integration Techniques

Finding antiderivatives takes pattern-matching, not a formula. These are the standard moves — and a reminder that some integrals have no elementary answer at all.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Unlike differentiation, integration has no mechanical recipe. These techniques are the toolkit, and they reveal that elementary antiderivatives are the exception, not the rule.

In the real world

The bell curve $e^{-x^2}$ has no elementary antiderivative, so statistics relies on numerical integration of it. Every p-value depends on that fact.

Story & open questions

Liouville proved in the 1830s that integrals like $\int e^{-x^2}$ cannot be written in elementary terms; the 1968 Risch algorithm lets computers decide when a closed form exists.

By the end you can
  • Integrate by substitution and by parts.
  • Decompose with partial fractions.
  • Recognize when a trigonometric substitution helps.

Substitution and by parts

Rules

$\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=\int f(u)\,du$ and $\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du$.

Example

$\int x e^x\,dx=xe^x-\int e^x\,dx=(x-1)e^x+C$ by parts.

Partial fractions and trig substitution

Example Partial fractions

$\int\dfrac{dx}{x^2-1}=\tfrac12\ln\left|\dfrac{x-1}{x+1}\right|+C$.

For $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$ use $x=a\sin\theta$; for $\sqrt{a^2+x^2}$, $x=a\tan\theta$.

Common pitfalls

• Convert everything (including $dx$) to the new variable.
• Change the limits or convert back — never mix.
• Partial fractions need a proper fraction; divide first if not.

Practice

Practice 1

$\int x\cos(x^2)\,dx$.

Answer
$u=x^2$: $\tfrac12\sin(x^2)+C$.
Practice 2

$\int \ln x\,dx$.

Answer
By parts: $x\ln x-x+C$.
Practice 3

$\int_0^1 x e^{-x}\,dx$.

Answer
$1-2e^{-1}$.
