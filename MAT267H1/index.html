MAT267H1: solutions you never write down.

The applied course asks you to solve differential equations. This one asks whether a solution exists, whether it is the only one, how far it extends, and what it does after a long time — questions you can answer for equations no formula will ever crack.

About this course

The theory course behind the solution methods.

MAT267H1 "Advanced Ordinary Differential Equations": first order equations and the standard integration tricks; existence and uniqueness by Picard iteration, continuation and dependence on initial data; linear systems, fundamental matrices and the matrix exponential; higher order equations, series solutions and regular singular points; then the qualitative half of the course — phase planes, linearisation, Lyapunov stability, limit cycles and Poincaré–Bendixson. Prerequisites: MAT159H1 (or MAT157Y1) and MAT247H1. The linear algebra earns its place immediately: a constant-coefficient system is solved by putting a matrix in Jordan form. This guide follows Teschl's Ordinary Differential Equations and Dynamical Systems, with Hirsch, Smale and Devaney for the geometry.

02

Textbook Spine

Teschl's book is free from the author's page and covers the whole course in the same order. Hirsch, Smale and Devaney is the standard reference for the qualitative half and is far more pictorial. Coddington and Levinson is where to go when a proof in the first half looks too quick. Check the current syllabus for the assigned text.

Unit-to-textbook alignment
Solving and proving

Units 1-3 pair with Teschl's opening chapters. Unit 1 is the only place the course looks like the applied version; from Unit 2 the subject is analysis, and the fixed point theorem does the work.

Linear theory

Units 4-7 are the linear-algebra chapters. Everything computable in the course lives here, and the matrix exponential is the single most useful object you will take away.

Qualitative theory

Units 8-10 pair with Hirsch–Smale–Devaney. No solution formulas at all: the questions are about long-run behaviour, and the answers come from geometry.

What the course leaves out

Bifurcation theory, chaos and higher-dimensional dynamics are MAT335H1's material. This course stops at the plane, where Poincaré–Bendixson still holds.

EXISTENCE

Prove it before solving it

The first question about any equation is whether a solution exists and is unique. Get used to checking the Lipschitz condition before you start integrating.

LINEARISE

Matrices decide everything

For linear systems the eigenvalues are the whole answer, and near an equilibrium a nonlinear system usually behaves like its linearisation. MAT247H1 is not a formality here.

PICTURE

Draw the phase plane

A sketch with nullclines and arrows answers most qualitative questions faster than any calculation, and it catches sign errors instantly.

ESTIMATE

Inequalities beat formulas

Gronwall, energy functions and comparison arguments give real conclusions about equations with no closed-form solution. That is the point of the whole course.

Practice Plan

A half-credit course that moves quickly. The unit pages carry the guided questions; use this mix across the term.

01

Two-column habit

For every equation write the solution method on one side and the existence-uniqueness justification on the other. Exams ask for both, and the second is where marks are lost.

02

Exponentials by hand

Compute $e^{At}$ for a diagonalisable matrix, a defective one, and a rotation. Three examples cover every case you will meet.

03

Sketch first

Before any phase-plane question, draw nullclines, mark equilibria, put arrows in each region. The classification then confirms a picture you already have.

04

Counterexample file

Non-unique solutions, finite-time blow-up, a centre that linearisation gets wrong. The theory is defined by what it cannot promise.

Course-wide challenge bank

Original problems in the style of a theory-track problem set. Write a full solution before opening the answer.

Challenge 1 · Uniqueness fails

Show $y'=y^{2/3}$, $y(0)=0$ has more than one solution, and say exactly which hypothesis breaks.

Solution sketch
$y\equiv0$ works, and so does $y=(t/3)^3$; you can even glue them, staying at zero until any time $c$ and then leaving. The right side is continuous, so existence holds, but $y^{2/3}$ is not Lipschitz at $0$ — its difference quotient blows up there — and uniqueness is exactly what the Lipschitz condition buys.
Challenge 2 · Finite-time blow-up

Solve $y'=y^2$, $y(0)=1$, and explain why a global solution was never promised.

Solution sketch
$y=1/(1-t)$, which escapes at $t=1$. Picard's theorem is local: it gives a solution on some interval around $0$, and the continuation theorem says the solution runs until it leaves every compact set. Here it leaves by going to infinity in finite time.
Challenge 3 · Defective matrix

Compute $e^{At}$ for $A=\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}$.

Solution sketch
Write $A=2I+N$ with $N=\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}$, $N^2=0$. The two commute, so $e^{At}=e^{2t}e^{Nt}=e^{2t}(I+Nt) =e^{2t}\begin{smallmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}$. The factor of $t$ is the signature of a repeated eigenvalue with only one eigenvector.
Challenge 4 · Linearisation is not enough

The system $x'=-y+ax(x^2+y^2)$, $y'=x+ay(x^2+y^2)$ linearises to a centre. What actually happens?

Solution sketch
In polar coordinates $r'=ar^3$ and $\theta'=1$. The origin is a stable spiral for $a<0$, an unstable spiral for $a>0$, and a true centre only when $a=0$. Purely imaginary eigenvalues are the case where linearisation decides nothing.
