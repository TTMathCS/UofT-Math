Transcendental Functions

The rigorous payoff: define $\ln$ as an integral, $\exp$ as its inverse, and recover all their laws — closing the first half of analysis with functions built on everything before.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Defining $e^x$ by "repeated multiplication" makes no sense for irrational exponents. Building $\ln$ as $\int_1^x\tfrac1t\,dt$ first gives a rigorous, gap-free construction of all the transcendental functions.

In the real world

$e^x$ governs growth and decay; logarithms scale pH, decibels, and information in bits; trig functions drive every Fourier method in audio, imaging, and signal processing.

Story & open questions

Napier built logarithms in 1614 to ease astronomers' arithmetic; Euler named $e$. Hermite and Lindemann later proved $e$ and $\pi$ transcendental, settling "squaring the circle" once and for all.

By the end you can
  • Define $\ln$ as an integral and prove the log laws.
  • Define $\exp$ as the inverse and find its derivative.
  • Differentiate trig and inverse-trig functions.

Logarithm and exponential

Definition

$\ln x=\displaystyle\int_1^x\frac{1}{t}\,dt$ for $x>0$, so $(\ln x)'= \tfrac1x$ by the FTC. Define $\exp=\ln^{-1}$ and $e=\exp(1)$; then $(e^x)'=e^x$.

Example $\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b$
Fix $a$; $g(x)=\ln(ax)$ has $g'(x)=\tfrac1x=(\ln x)'$.
So $g(x)-\ln x$ is constant, equal to $\ln a$ at $x=1$. ∎

Trigonometric functions

Key limit

$\lim_{x\to0}\dfrac{\sin x}{x}=1$, from which $(\sin x)'=\cos x$ and $(\cos x)'=-\sin x$.

Example

$\dfrac{d}{dx}\arctan x=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}$ via the inverse-function rule of Unit 8.

Common pitfalls

• $\dfrac{d}{dx}a^x=a^x\ln a$, not $x a^{x-1}$.
• Keep domains straight: $\ln$ needs $x>0$, $\arcsin$ needs $|x|\le1$.
• The integral definition of $\ln$ is what makes irrational exponents meaningful.

Practice

Practice 1

Prove $\ln(x^r)=r\ln x$ for rational $r$.

Hint
Differentiate both sides; they share derivative $r/x$ and agree at $x=1$.
Practice 2

Differentiate $y=x^x$ ($x>0$).

Answer
$\ln y=x\ln x$, so $y'=x^x(\ln x+1)$.
Practice 3

Compute $\lim_{x\to0}\dfrac{e^x-1}{x}$ from a derivative.

Answer
It is $(e^x)'$ at $0$, i.e. $1$.
