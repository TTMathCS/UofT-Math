Polynomials over a Field

Polynomial arithmetic is the algebra behind characteristic and minimal polynomials. Division, greatest common divisors, and factorization explain which polynomial identities a linear map can satisfy.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Polynomials over a field behave much like integers: they admit division with remainder, greatest common divisors, and unique factorization. This structure makes eigenvalue and minimal-polynomial arguments possible.

In the real world

Polynomial arithmetic over finite fields drives error-correcting codes, cryptography, and symbolic computation. Resultants let software test whether two polynomial equations share a root without solving them first.

Course connection

The characteristic polynomial detects eigenvalues. The minimal polynomial records the lowest-degree relation satisfied by a matrix and gives a sharper test for diagonalizability.

By the end you can
  • Divide polynomials over a field and apply the remainder theorem.
  • Compute a polynomial gcd with the Euclidean algorithm.
  • Use unique factorization and understand why the field matters.
  • Define minimal polynomials and resultants.

Division and roots

Division algorithm

For $f,g\in F[x]$ with $g\ne0$, there are unique $q,r\in F[x]$ such that $f=qg+r$ and either $r=0$ or $\deg r<\deg g$.

Remainder and factor theorems

The remainder when $f(x)$ is divided by $x-a$ is $f(a)$. Therefore $a\in F$ is a root of $f$ exactly when $x-a$ divides $f$.

Example

Over any field, $x^3-1=(x-1)(x^2+x+1)$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ it splits into three linear factors; over $\mathbb{R}$ the quadratic factor is irreducible.

GCD and unique factorization

Euclidean algorithm

Repeated polynomial division computes a greatest common divisor. Moreover, $\gcd(f,g)$ can be written as $af+bg$ for some $a,b\in F[x]$.

Unique factorization

Every nonzero nonconstant polynomial in $F[x]$ factors into irreducible polynomials, uniquely up to order and multiplication by nonzero constants.

Minimal polynomial and resultant

Definition Minimal polynomial

For a linear operator $T$ on a finite-dimensional space, the minimal polynomial $m_T$ is the unique monic polynomial of least positive degree with $m_T(T)=0$. It divides every polynomial that annihilates $T$, including the characteristic polynomial.

Diagonalization test

Over a field where the characteristic polynomial splits, $T$ is diagonalizable exactly when $m_T$ is a product of distinct linear factors.

Definition Resultant

The resultant $\operatorname{Res}(f,g)$ is a determinant built from the coefficients of $f$ and $g$. It is zero exactly when the polynomials share a root over an algebraic closure.

Common pitfalls

• Factorization depends on the field: $x^2+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$ but splits over $\mathbb{C}$.
• A gcd is unique only after choosing it monic; nonzero scalar multiples are also common divisors.
• The minimal polynomial and characteristic polynomial need not be equal.
• $p(T)=0$ means the zero operator, not that $p$ is the zero polynomial.

Practice

Practice 1

Divide $x^3+2x^2-1$ by $x+1$.

Answer
The quotient is $x^2+x-1$ and the remainder is $0$.
Practice 2

Find $\gcd(x^3-x,\,x^2-1)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.

Answer
$x^2-1$, since $x^3-x=x(x^2-1)$.
Practice 3

Factor $x^2+1$ over $\mathbb{R}$ and over $\mathbb{C}$.

Answer
It is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$ and equals $(x-i)(x+i)$ over $\mathbb{C}$.
Practice 4

Find the minimal polynomial of $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

Answer
$m_A(x)=x-2$, while the characteristic polynomial is $(x-2)^2$.
Practice 5

Why is a matrix with minimal polynomial $(x-1)^2$ not diagonalizable?

Answer
The minimal polynomial has a repeated linear factor; a diagonalizable operator has a square-free minimal polynomial.
