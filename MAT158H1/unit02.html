The Real Numbers & Least Upper Bounds

What separates $\mathbb{R}$ from $\mathbb{Q}$ is completeness — no gaps. The least upper bound axiom is the single foundation every deep theorem of analysis stands on.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

$\mathbb{Q}$ has holes — there is no rational whose square is $2$. Analysis needs a number line with no gaps so that limits actually land somewhere. Completeness is exactly that guarantee.

In the real world

Floating-point arithmetic and numerical analysis constantly wrestle with the gap between true real numbers and what a computer can store. Understanding completeness clarifies why rounding errors and convergence behave as they do.

Story & open questions

Dedekind (with "cuts") and Cantor (with Cauchy sequences) independently constructed $\mathbb{R}$ in the 1870s — finally giving a precise definition to the objects calculus had used for two hundred years.

By the end you can

  • Define upper bound, supremum, and infimum.
  • State and use the Least Upper Bound axiom.
  • Derive the Archimedean property from completeness.

Bounds and the supremum

Definition Supremum

$M$ is an upper bound of $S$ if $x\le M$ for all $x\in S$. The supremum $\sup S$ is the least upper bound: an upper bound that is $\le$ every other upper bound. The infimum $\inf S$ is the greatest lower bound.

Example

$S=\{1-\tfrac1n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$ has $\sup S=1$, even though $1\notin S$. The sup need not be attained.

The completeness axiom

Axiom Least Upper Bound

Every nonempty subset of $\mathbb{R}$ that is bounded above has a supremum in $\mathbb{R}$. This single axiom is what $\mathbb{Q}$ lacks.

It is the engine behind the Intermediate Value Theorem, the Extreme Value Theorem, and the convergence of monotone sequences — all proved in later units.

The Archimedean property

Theorem

For any real $x$ there is a natural number $n>x$. Equivalently, for any $\varepsilon>0$ there is $n$ with $\tfrac1n<\varepsilon$.

Proof idea
If $\mathbb{N}$ were bounded above it would have a supremum $s$; then $s-1$ is not an upper bound, so some $n>s-1$, giving $n+1>s$ — a contradiction.
Common pitfalls

• The supremum may not belong to the set; "max" requires it to be attained.
• Completeness is about upper bounds existing as real numbers — that is what fails in $\mathbb{Q}$.
• To show $s=\sup S$: prove $s$ is an upper bound and that $s-\varepsilon$ is not, for every $\varepsilon>0$.

Practice

Practice 1

Find $\sup$ and $\inf$ of $S=\{\tfrac1n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$.

Answer
$\sup S=1$ (attained), $\inf S=0$ (not attained).
Practice 2

Prove that if $s=\sup S$ then for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $x\in S$ with $x>s-\varepsilon$.

Hint
Else $s-\varepsilon$ is an upper bound smaller than $s$, contradicting "least."
Practice 3

Use the Archimedean property to show $\inf\{\tfrac1n\}=0$.

Hint
$0$ is a lower bound; for any $\varepsilon>0$ some $\tfrac1n<\varepsilon$, so nothing larger than $0$ is a lower bound.
