The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

The theorem that differentiation and integration are inverse operations — proved here from the Riemann definition and the Mean Value Theorem.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Two operations invented for unrelated reasons — slopes and areas — turn out to be exact inverses. That is what lets us integrate by finding antiderivatives instead of summing forever.

In the real world

It is what makes differential equations solvable — the models behind orbits, circuits, epidemics, and the Black–Scholes option price all rely on it.

Story & open questions

Newton's teacher Isaac Barrow glimpsed the link geometrically; Newton and Leibniz saw it in full. This is the result that turned calculus from a bag of tricks into the central tool of science.

By the end you can
  • State and prove both parts of the FTC.
  • Evaluate integrals via antiderivatives.
  • Differentiate integrals with variable limits.

Part 1 — the area function

Theorem

If $f$ is continuous and $F(x)=\int_a^x f$, then $F'(x)=f(x)$.

Proof idea
$\dfrac{F(x+h)-F(x)}{h}=\dfrac1h\int_x^{x+h}f$ is an average of $f$ over $[x,x+h]$.
Continuity forces that average to $f(x)$ as $h\to0$. ∎

Part 2 — evaluation

Theorem

If $f$ is continuous and $G'=f$, then $\int_a^b f=G(b)-G(a)$.

Variable limits

$\dfrac{d}{dx}\int_a^{g(x)}f(t)\,dt=f(g(x))g'(x)$ by the chain rule.

Common pitfalls

• In Part 1 the variable is the upper limit; the dummy variable inside is different.
• With a variable upper limit $g(x)$, keep the $g'(x)$ factor.
• Part 2 needs $f$ continuous and $G$ a genuine antiderivative on $[a,b]$.

Practice

Practice 1

Compute $\dfrac{d}{dx}\int_0^{x^2}\cos t\,dt$.

Answer
$\cos(x^2)\cdot 2x$.
Practice 2

Evaluate $\int_1^e\dfrac{\ln x}{x}\,dx$.

Answer
Antiderivative $\tfrac12(\ln x)^2$, value $\tfrac12$.
Practice 3

If $\int_0^x f=x e^x$, find $f$.

Answer
Differentiate: $f(x)=(1+x)e^x$.
