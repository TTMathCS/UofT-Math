Integration Methods

A toolkit for finding antiderivatives: reverse the chain rule (substitution), reverse the product rule (by parts), and break rational functions apart (partial fractions).

By the end you can
  • Integrate by substitution and adjust the limits.
  • Integrate by parts and pick $u$ and $dv$ well.
  • Decompose a rational function into partial fractions.
  • Recognize when a trigonometric substitution helps.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Unlike derivatives, antiderivatives have no mechanical recipe — they take pattern-matching and cleverness. These techniques are the standard moves. And some functions, it turns out, have no elementary antiderivative at all.

In the real world

The bell curve $e^{-x^2}$ — the most important function in statistics — has no elementary antiderivative, so its integral must be computed numerically. Every statistical table and every p-value traces back to that fact.

Story & open questions

Liouville proved in the 1830s that integrals like $\int e^{-x^2}$ genuinely cannot be written in elementary terms — a rare and surprising "this is impossible" theorem. In 1968 the Risch algorithm turned the question into an algorithmic one for broad classes of elementary functions, allowing computer algebra systems to decide many cases.

Choosing a method

Integration technique is pattern recognition plus verification. Always check the derivative of your answer; that habit catches sign errors, missing constants, and wrong substitutions quickly.

Decision guide
Use substitution when you see a composition and the derivative of the inside is also present.
Use integration by parts when the integrand is a product and differentiating one factor makes it simpler.
Use partial fractions for rational functions after ensuring the numerator degree is smaller than the denominator degree.
Use trigonometric substitution when a square root matches $a^2-x^2$, $a^2+x^2$, or $x^2-a^2$.

Definite integral discipline

For definite integrals, every symbol must describe the same variable. After substituting $u=g(x)$, either change the bounds immediately or convert the antiderivative back to $x$ before evaluating.

  • Changed bounds method: $x=a$ becomes $u=g(a)$ and $x=b$ becomes $u=g(b)$.
  • Back-substitution method: find an antiderivative in $x$, then plug in the original $x$-bounds.
  • Never plug $x$-bounds into an expression that is still written in $u$.

Substitution

Rule

$\displaystyle\int f\!\big(g(x)\big)g'(x)\,dx=\int f(u)\,du$ with $u=g(x)$. For definite integrals, change the limits to $g(a)$ and $g(b)$.

Example

$\displaystyle\int 2x\,e^{x^2}\,dx$. Let $u=x^2$, $du=2x\,dx$: $\int e^u\,du=e^{x^2}+C$.

Integration by parts

Rule

$\displaystyle\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du.$ A good guide for choosing $u$ is LIATE (log, inverse-trig, algebraic, trig, exponential).

Example

$\displaystyle\int x e^{x}\,dx$. Take $u=x$, $dv=e^x dx$: $=xe^x-\int e^x\,dx=xe^x-e^x+C.$

Partial fractions

A proper rational function can be decomposed into simpler fractions. Repeated linear factors require a term for every power; irreducible quadratic factors require linear numerators, again with terms for repeated powers.

Example

$\displaystyle\int\frac{dx}{x^2-1}$. Write $\dfrac{1}{x^2-1}= \dfrac{1/2}{x-1}-\dfrac{1/2}{x+1}$, so the integral is $\tfrac12\ln\left|\dfrac{x-1}{x+1}\right|+C$.

Trigonometric substitution

For $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$ use $x=a\sin\theta$; for $\sqrt{a^2+x^2}$ use $x=a\tan\theta$; for $\sqrt{x^2-a^2}$ use $x=a\sec\theta$.

Example

$\displaystyle\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}=\arcsin x+C$ — directly, or via $x=\sin\theta$, on an interval inside $(-1,1)$.

Common pitfalls

• After substitution, convert everything (including $dx$) to the new variable.
• In definite integrals, either change the limits or convert back before plugging in — don't mix.
• Partial fractions need a proper fraction first; if not, do polynomial division.

Practice

Problems adapted from the MAT137 question bank by Alfonso Gracia-Saz, used under CC BY-SA 4.0. Write full solutions before opening the answer.

Practice 1 · Substitution

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int x\cos(x^2)\,dx$.

Answer
$u=x^2$: $\tfrac12\sin(x^2)+C$.
Practice 2 · By parts

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \ln x\,dx$.

Hint
By parts with $u=\ln x$, $dv=dx$: $x\ln x-x+C$.
Practice 3 · Partial fractions

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int\frac{3x+1}{x^2+x}\,dx$.

Hint
$\dfrac{3x+1}{x(x+1)}= \dfrac{1}{x}+\dfrac{2}{x+1}$, giving $\ln|x|+2\ln|x+1|+C$.
Practice 4 · By parts

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{1} x\,e^{-x}\,dx$.

Answer
By parts: $[-(x+1)e^{-x}]_0^1=1-2e^{-1}.$
Practice 5 · By parts twice

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int x^2\sin x\,dx$.

Answer
$-x^2\cos x+2x\sin x+2\cos x+C.$
Practice 6 · Power reduction

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \cos^2 x\,dx$.

Hint
$\cos^2x=\tfrac{1+\cos2x}{2}$, so the integral is $\tfrac x2+\tfrac{\sin2x}{4}+C$.
Practice 7 · Trig substitution

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1\sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx$.

Answer
Let $x=\sin\theta$; the integral becomes $\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta=\tfrac\pi4$ (a quarter disk).
Practice 8 · Partial fractions

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{x^3-x}\,dx$.

Hint
$\dfrac1{x(x-1)(x+1)}= \dfrac{-1}{x}+\dfrac{1}{2(x-1)}+\dfrac{1}{2(x+1)}$, so the integral is $-\ln|x|+\tfrac12\ln|x-1|+\tfrac12\ln|x+1|+C.$
