Integration Techniques

Finding antiderivatives takes pattern-matching, not a formula. These are the standard moves — substitution, parts, partial fractions, and trigonometric substitution — and a reminder that some integrals have no elementary answer at all.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Unlike differentiation, integration has no mechanical recipe: there is no "product rule for antiderivatives." These techniques are the toolkit built up over centuries of trial, and they reveal that elementary antiderivatives are the exception, not the rule.

In the real world

The bell curve $e^{-x^2}$ has no elementary antiderivative, so statistics relies on numerical integration of it. Every p-value depends on that fact. Partial fractions decompositions are also exactly how control theory inverts Laplace transforms.

Story & open questions

Liouville proved in the 1830s that integrals like $\int e^{-x^2}$ cannot be written in elementary terms; the 1968 Risch algorithm lets computers decide when a closed form exists at all — a rare case of a fully mechanical answer to "can this be done by hand?"

By the end you can
  • Integrate by substitution and by parts, including repeated and circular applications.
  • Decompose any rational function with partial fractions — linear, repeated, and irreducible quadratic factors.
  • Recognize when a trigonometric substitution or a power-reduction identity helps.
  • Explain, with an example, why "elementary antiderivative" is not guaranteed.

Substitution and by parts

Rules

$\displaystyle\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=\int f(u)\,du$ (substitution, $u=g(x)$) and $\displaystyle\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du$ (parts, from the product rule run backwards).

Example Basic parts

$\int x e^x\,dx$: let $u=x,\ dv=e^x dx$, so $du=dx,\ v=e^x$.

$\int x e^x\,dx = xe^x-\int e^x\,dx=(x-1)e^x+C$.
Example Repeated parts

$\int x^2 e^x\,dx$.

First pass: $u=x^2,\ dv=e^xdx$ gives $x^2e^x-2\int xe^x\,dx$.
Reuse the previous example: $=x^2e^x-2(x-1)e^x+C= (x^2-2x+2)e^x+C$.
Example Circular parts

$\int e^x\cos x\,dx$.

Parts twice ($u=\cos x$ then $u=\sin x$) returns the same integral $I$ on the right: $I=e^x\cos x+e^x\sin x-I$.
Solve for $I$: $2I=e^x(\cos x+\sin x)$, so $I=\tfrac12e^x(\cos x+\sin x)+C$.
Which $u$ to pick? The mnemonic LIATE (Logarithmic, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trigonometric, Exponential) orders good choices of $u$ for parts — pick $u$ from as far left in that list as appears, since it differentiates toward something simpler while $dv$ integrates easily.

Partial fractions

Any proper rational function $P(x)/Q(x)$ (degree of $P$ less than degree of $Q$) splits into a sum of simpler pieces, one for each factor of $Q$.

The three cases

A factor $(x-a)$ contributes $\dfrac{A}{x-a}$; a repeated factor $(x-a)^k$ contributes $\dfrac{A_1}{x-a}+\cdots+\dfrac{A_k}{(x-a)^k}$; an irreducible quadratic $(x^2+bx+c)$ contributes $\dfrac{Ax+B}{x^2+bx+c}$.

Example Distinct linear factors

$\dfrac{1}{x^2-1}=\dfrac{A}{x-1}+\dfrac{B}{x+1}$.

$1=A(x+1)+B(x-1)$. Set $x=1$: $A=\tfrac12$. Set $x=-1$: $B=-\tfrac12$.
$\int\dfrac{dx}{x^2-1}=\tfrac12\ln|x-1|-\tfrac12\ln|x+1|+C =\tfrac12\ln\left|\dfrac{x-1}{x+1}\right|+C$.
Example Repeated factor

$\dfrac{x}{(x-1)^2}=\dfrac{A}{x-1}+\dfrac{B}{(x-1)^2}$.

$x=A(x-1)+B$. Set $x=1$: $B=1$. Match $x$-coefficients: $A=1$.
$\int\dfrac{x\,dx}{(x-1)^2}=\ln|x-1|-\dfrac{1}{x-1}+C$.
Example Irreducible quadratic

$\dfrac{1}{x(x^2+1)}=\dfrac{A}{x}+\dfrac{Bx+C}{x^2+1}$.

$1=A(x^2+1)+(Bx+C)x$. Set $x=0$: $A=1$. Matching $x^2$-coefficients: $0=A+B\Rightarrow B=-1$; matching $x$-coefficients: $C=0$.
$\int\dfrac{dx}{x(x^2+1)}=\ln|x|-\tfrac12\ln(x^2+1)+C$.
What if the fraction isn't proper? Divide first. $\dfrac{x^3}{x-1}=x^2+x+1+\dfrac{1}{x-1}$ by polynomial long division, and only the remainder term needs partial fractions.

Trigonometric substitution and trigonometric integrals

The three substitutions

For $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$ use $x=a\sin\theta$; for $\sqrt{a^2+x^2}$ use $x=a\tan\theta$; for $\sqrt{x^2-a^2}$ use $x=a\sec\theta$. Each turns the radical into a single trig factor via a Pythagorean identity.

Example

$\int\dfrac{dx}{\sqrt{4-x^2}}$, using $x=2\sin\theta$, $dx=2\cos\theta\, d\theta$, $\sqrt{4-x^2}=2\cos\theta$.

$\int\dfrac{2\cos\theta\,d\theta}{2\cos\theta}=\theta+C= \arcsin\!\left(\dfrac{x}{2}\right)+C$.
Example Power-reduction

$\int\sin^2x\,dx$, using $\sin^2x=\tfrac12(1-\cos2x)$.

$=\tfrac12x-\tfrac14\sin2x+C$.

For $\int\sin^mx\cos^nx\,dx$: if either power is odd, peel off one factor and substitute with the other function; if both are even, use the half-angle identities repeatedly.

Common pitfalls

• Convert everything — including $dx$ — to the new variable; do not leave a mix of $x$ and $u$.
• With definite integrals under substitution, either change the limits or convert back before evaluating — never mix the two.
• Partial fractions require a proper fraction; divide first if the numerator's degree is not smaller.
• After a trig substitution, convert back to $x$ using a reference triangle, including for $\theta$ that appears alone (as $\arcsin$, $\arctan$, etc.).
• Not every elementary function has an elementary antiderivative — do not hunt forever for a closed form that provably does not exist.

Practice

Work each one fully by hand before checking.

Practice 1 · Substitution

$\int x\cos(x^2)\,dx$.

Answer
$u=x^2,\ du=2x\,dx$: $\tfrac12\sin(x^2)+C$.
Practice 2 · By parts

$\int \ln x\,dx$.

Answer
$u=\ln x,\ dv=dx$: $x\ln x-x+C$.
Practice 3 · Definite integral

$\int_0^1 x e^{-x}\,dx$.

Answer
By parts: $[-xe^{-x}-e^{-x}]_0^1=1-2e^{-1}$.
Practice 4 · Repeated linear factors

$\int\dfrac{dx}{x(x-1)^2}$.

Solution
$\dfrac{1}{x(x-1)^2}=\dfrac{1}{x}-\dfrac{1}{x-1}+\dfrac{1}{(x-1)^2}$, so the integral is $\ln|x|-\ln|x-1|-\dfrac{1}{x-1}+C$.
Practice 5 · Irreducible quadratic

$\int\dfrac{dx}{x^2+4}$.

Answer
$\tfrac12\arctan(x/2)+C$.
Practice 6 · Trig substitution

$\int\dfrac{dx}{x^2\sqrt{x^2+9}}$.

Solution
$x=3\tan\theta$ gives $\int\dfrac{\cos\theta}{9\sin^2\theta}\,d\theta= -\dfrac{1}{9\sin\theta}+C=-\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2+9}}{9x}+C$.
Practice 7 · Circular parts

$\int e^{2x}\sin x\,dx$.

Solution
Parts twice gives $I=e^{2x}(2\sin x-\cos x)-4I$ up to sign bookkeeping; solving, $I=\tfrac15e^{2x}(2\sin x-\cos x)+C$.
Practice 8 · No elementary form

Why can't you find a closed-form antiderivative of $\int\dfrac{\sin x}{x}\,dx$ by these techniques, even though the function is perfectly smooth?

Answer
Liouville's theory shows $\operatorname{Si}(x)=\int_0^x\tfrac{\sin t}{t}dt$ is provably not expressible in elementary terms — no substitution or parts trick will ever produce one, no matter how cleverly applied.
