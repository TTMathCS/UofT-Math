Logic, Sets & Proof

Analysis begins with airtight reasoning. This unit is the proof language — quantifiers and proof methods — on which every later theorem about the real numbers rests.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

In analysis "obvious" is not allowed. Statements must be proved from axioms, because intuition about infinity and limits is famously unreliable — exactly where 18th-century calculus kept going wrong.

In the real world

This is the logic that runs computers and the basis of proof assistants like Lean and Coq, now used to verify major theorems and safety-critical software line by line.

Story & open questions

The 19th-century drive for rigour came precisely because calculus had been caught being sloppy. Boole, Cantor, Russell, and Gödel turned reasoning itself into an object of study; Gödel's 1931 theorems showed every rich system has true statements it cannot prove.

By the end you can
  • Read and negate statements with $\forall$ and $\exists$.
  • Use the contrapositive and proof by contradiction.
  • Prove statements by induction and strong induction.
  • Write a clean proof and spot a broken one.

Quantifiers and negation

Definition Quantifiers

$\forall x\in S,\ P(x)$: true for every $x$. $\exists x\in S,\ P(x)$: true for at least one $x$. Negation flips each quantifier and negates the inside: $\neg(\forall x\,P)\equiv\exists x\,\neg P$.

Order matters: $\forall x\,\exists y$ and $\exists y\,\forall x$ usually mean different things. Mastering this is the whole game in ε–δ arguments.

Proof methods

The toolkit

Direct ($P\Rightarrow Q$), contrapositive ($\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P$), contradiction (assume false, derive an impossibility), and induction (for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$). An implication and its contrapositive are always equivalent.

Example Contradiction

$\sqrt2$ is irrational: if $\sqrt2=p/q$ in lowest terms, then $p^2=2q^2$ makes $p$ even, then $q$ even — contradicting "lowest terms."

Induction

Principle

If $P(1)$ holds and $P(k)\Rightarrow P(k+1)$ for all $k$, then $P(n)$ holds for all $n$. Strong induction may assume $P(1),\dots,P(k)$ all at once.

Example

$1+2+\cdots+n=\tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$: base $n=1$ holds; the step adds $(k+1)$ to $\tfrac{k(k+1)}{2}$ to get $\tfrac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}$.

Common pitfalls

• Proving the converse by accident — check the direction.
• One counterexample kills a $\forall$; one example proves an $\exists$.
• In induction, never skip the base case or forget to use the hypothesis.

Practice

Practice 1

Prove: if $x^2$ is irrational then $x$ is irrational.

Solution
Contrapositive: $x$ rational $\Rightarrow x^2=p^2/q^2$ rational.
Practice 2

Negate: "$\exists M\ \forall n,\ a_n\le M$."

Answer
"$\forall M\ \exists n,\ a_n>M$" — the sequence is unbounded above.
Practice 3

Prove $n<2^n$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$ by induction.

Hint
Step: $k+1<2^k+1\le 2^k+2^k=2^{k+1}$.
