Derivatives

The derivative is a limit. Once limits are solid, the derivative and all its rules follow — and in MAT137 you prove the rules, not just apply them.

By the end you can
  • Compute derivatives from the limit definition.
  • Prove that differentiability implies continuity.
  • Prove and apply the sum, product, quotient, and chain rules.
  • Differentiate implicitly.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

An average rate of change loses the moment. The derivative captures the instantaneous one — the exact velocity now, the marginal cost of the next unit, the slope right here.

In the real world

Derivatives are everywhere modern: physics (velocity, acceleration), economics (marginal analysis), finance (the option "Greeks"), and above all machine learning, where most modern neural networks are trained with gradient-based optimization.

Story & open questions

Newton and Leibniz discovered it independently, then feuded bitterly over credit and helping isolate parts of British mathematics from Continental developments. Leibniz's $dy/dx$ notation became standard. Today "automatic differentiation" computes these derivatives at enormous scale.

The learning spine

Every derivative calculation in this unit is a disguised limit. Rules like product, quotient, and chain rules are shortcuts only after their hypotheses are met. When learning a new rule, first understand the limit proof, then practice recognizing the pattern quickly.

Derivative-from-definition routine
Write the correct quotient: $\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$ or $\dfrac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}$.
Simplify before taking the limit. Usually this means factoring, expanding, rationalizing, or combining fractions.
Only after the expression is defined for $h\ne0$ and simplified should you let $h\to0$.

Proofs you should own

MAT137 treats the differentiation rules as theorems, not magic formulas. A student who can reproduce the following proof ideas is ready for later analysis.

  • Differentiable implies continuous. Write the change in $f$ as a difference quotient times the change in $x$.
  • Product rule. Add and subtract the same middle term so the difference quotient splits into two known limits.
  • Quotient rule. Derive it from the product rule and the derivative of the reciprocal.
  • Chain rule. Introduce the inner change $g(x+h)-g(x)$ and handle the case where that change may be zero.

The definition

Definition Derivative

$f$ is differentiable at $a$ if the limit $$f'(a)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$$ exists. Geometrically it is the slope of the tangent line at $\big(a,f(a)\big)$. Equivalently, $f'(a)=\lim_{x\to a}\dfrac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}$.

A secant line becoming the tangent line

For $f(x)=x^2$ at $a=1$, the secant slope is $\frac{(1+h)^2-1}{h}=2+h$. As $h\to0$, it approaches the tangent slope $2$.

h = 0.90 secant slope = 2.90
Example From the definition

For $f(x)=\tfrac1x$, $a\ne0$:

$\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}=\dfrac{1}{h}\!\left(\dfrac{1}{a+h}- \dfrac1a\right)=\dfrac{-1}{a(a+h)}.$
As $h\to0$ this is $-\dfrac1{a^2}$, so $f'(a)=-1/a^2$.
Example A corner is not differentiable

For $f(x)=|x|$ at $0$, the difference quotient is $|h|/h$. It tends to $-1$ from the left and $1$ from the right, so the two-sided derivative does not exist.

Differentiability implies continuity

Theorem

If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$. (The converse fails: $|x|$ is continuous but not differentiable at $0$.)

Proof idea
$f(x)-f(a)=\dfrac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}\cdot(x-a)$ for $x\ne a$.
As $x\to a$ the first factor $\to f'(a)$ and the second $\to0$, so $f(x)-f(a)\to0$, i.e. $f(x)\to f(a)$. ∎
Do not reverse the theorem Differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous, but continuous $\not\Rightarrow$ differentiable. Corners, cusps, vertical tangents, and sufficiently irregular oscillation can all destroy differentiability.

The differentiation rules

Theorem Rules

For differentiable functions, $(f+g)'=f'+g'$,   $(fg)'=f'g+fg'$,   $\left(\dfrac fg\right)'=\dfrac{f'g-fg'}{g^2}$ where $g\ne0$,   and the chain rule $(f\circ g)'(x)=f'\!\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x)$ when the composition is defined.

Example Product rule from the definition
$\dfrac{f(x+h)g(x+h)-f(x)g(x)}{h}$
$=\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\,g(x+h)+f(x)\,\dfrac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}.$
Let $h\to0$: $g(x+h)\to g(x)$ (continuity), giving $f'g+fg'$. ∎
Example Chain rule

$\dfrac{d}{dx}\,(x^2+1)^{10}=10(x^2+1)^9\cdot 2x.$

Proof idea Why the chain rule needs care

For $u=g(x+h)-g(x)$, one wants to split the quotient as

$\dfrac{f(g(x+h))-f(g(x))}{g(x+h)-g(x)} \cdot\dfrac{g(x+h)-g(x)}h.$
As $h\to0$, the factors approach $f'(g(x))$ and $g'(x)$. The subtlety is that $u$ may equal $0$ for some $h$. A complete proof extends the first quotient by defining its value at $u=0$ to be $f'(g(x))$; that extended function is continuous at $0$.

Local linear approximation

Differentiability says more than "a slope exists": near $a$, the function is well approximated by its tangent line.

Linearization

$f(a+h)=f(a)+f'(a)h+r(h)$, where $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{r(h)}h=0$. Informally, $f(a+h)\approx f(a)+f'(a)h$ for small $h$.

Example Estimate without a calculator

For $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ at $a=4$, $f(4)=2$ and $f'(4)=1/4$. Thus $\sqrt{4.1}\approx 2+\frac14(0.1)=2.025$.

Implicit differentiation

When $y$ is defined implicitly by an equation, differentiate both sides in $x$, treating $y=y(x)$ and using the chain rule, then solve for $y'$.

Example

For $x^2+y^2=1$: differentiate to get $2x+2yy'=0$, so $y'=-x/y$ where $y\ne0$. At $(\pm1,0)$ the circle has a vertical tangent, so $y$ is not locally a differentiable function of $x$ there.

Common pitfalls

• Treating $\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$ as $0/0$ — it is a limit, evaluate it.
• Dropping the inner factor $g'(x)$ in the chain rule.
• Using a rule before checking its hypotheses, especially dividing by a quantity that may be zero.
• Assuming continuous $\Rightarrow$ differentiable. It is the other direction.

Practice

Problems adapted from the MAT137 question bank by Alfonso Gracia-Saz, used under CC BY-SA 4.0. Write full solutions before opening the answer.

Practice 1 · Tangent line

What is the equation of the line tangent to $y=x$ at the point with $x$-coordinate $7$?

Answer
The graph is already a line, so it is its own tangent: $y=x$. (Check: slope $1$, passes through $(7,7)$.)
Practice 2 · Definition

From the definition, compute $f'(a)$ for $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$, $a>0$.

Solution
Rationalize: $\dfrac{\sqrt{a+h}-\sqrt a}{h}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{a+h}+\sqrt a}\to \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt a}.$
Practice 3 · Definition again

Compute $g'(4)$ directly from the definition for $g(x)=\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{x}}$.

Solution
$g(4)=1$, and $\dfrac{g(4+h)-g(4)}{h}=\dfrac{2-\sqrt{4+h}}{h\sqrt{4+h}} =\dfrac{-1}{\sqrt{4+h}\,(2+\sqrt{4+h})}\to\dfrac{-1}{2\cdot4}=-\dfrac18.$
Practice 4 · Theory

Which implication is true: continuous $\Rightarrow$ differentiable, or differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous? Justify.

Answer
Only differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous. The converse fails: $|x|$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable there.
Practice 5 · A corner

Let $h(x)=x|x|$. Compute $h'(0)$ from the definition.

Solution
$\dfrac{h(t)-h(0)}{t} =\dfrac{t|t|}{t}=|t|\to0$, so $h'(0)=0$. (Indeed $h'(x)=2|x|$.)
Practice 6 · Chain rule

Differentiate $f(x)=\tan(3x^2+1)$ and justify each step.

Answer
Chain rule: $f'(x)=\sec^2(3x^2+1)\cdot 6x$.
Practice 7 · Quotient rule

Prove the quotient rule from the product and chain rules.

Hint
Write $f/g=f\cdot(1/g)$, use the product rule and $(1/g)'=-g'/g^2$ (apply the chain rule to the reciprocal function $r(u)=1/u$): $(f/g)'=\dfrac{f'g-fg'}{g^2}.$
Practice 8 · Implicit

Find $y'$ if $x^3+y^3=6xy$ (the folium).

Answer
$3x^2+3y^2y'=6y+6xy'$, so $y'=\dfrac{6y-3x^2}{3y^2-6x}=\dfrac{2y-x^2}{y^2-2x}.$

Deep practice extensions

Use these after the main practice set. They are designed to force definition-level reasoning, theorem selection, and mixed application rather than routine calculation only.

Extension 1 · Definition computation

Compute the derivative of $f(x)=x^2$ at $a$ from the limit definition.

Guide
Use $[(a+h)^2-a^2]/h=2a+h$, whose limit as $h o0$ is $2a$.
Extension 2 · Proof rule

Prove the product rule assuming $f$ and $g$ are differentiable at $a$.

Guide
Add and subtract $f(a+h)g(a)$ in the difference quotient, split into two terms, and use continuity of $f$ or $g$ that follows from differentiability.
Extension 3 · Non-differentiable point

Show $|x|$ is not differentiable at $0$.

Guide
The right difference quotient is $1$ and the left difference quotient is $-1$, so the derivative limit does not exist.
Extension 4 · Linear approximation

Use differentials to estimate $\sqrt{4.04}$.

Guide
For $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ at $a=4$, $f(4)=2$ and $f\prime(4)=1/4$. The estimate is $2+(1/4)(0.04)=2.01$.
