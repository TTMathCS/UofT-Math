The Three Hard Theorems

Intermediate Value, Boundedness, and Extreme Value — the theorems that make calculus work. Each looks obvious, and each genuinely needs the completeness of $\mathbb{R}$ from Unit 2. This unit is where the least upper bound axiom earns its keep.

By the end you can
  • Prove the Intermediate Value Theorem from completeness.
  • Explain the proofs of the Boundedness and Extreme Value theorems.
  • Use the theorems to produce roots, fixed points, and extrema.
  • Give counterexamples showing every hypothesis is needed.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

"A continuous function that changes sign has a root" and "a continuous function on a closed interval attains a maximum" feel obvious — but over $\mathbb{Q}$ both are false, and proving them over $\mathbb{R}$ is the whole point of building the reals carefully.

In the real world

The IVT guarantees that root-finding by bisection converges; the EVT guarantees that optimization problems actually have a solution to find. Both are silent assumptions in countless algorithms.

Story & open questions

Bolzano proved the IVT rigorously in 1817, decades ahead of his time and largely unread. The companion Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem (every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence) carries both their names.

Why $\mathbb{Q}$ breaks all three

Take $f(x)=x^2-2$ on rational $[0,2]$. It is continuous, negative at $0$, positive at $2$ — and has no rational root: the graph slips through the hole at $\sqrt2$. Or take $g(x)=\dfrac{1}{x^2-2}$ on the same rational interval: continuous everywhere it is defined, yet unbounded. Completeness is not decoration; it is the load-bearing wall.

The Intermediate Value Theorem

Theorem IVT

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $y$ lies between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then $f(c)=y$ for some $c\in[a,b]$.

Proof (for $f(a)<0<f(b)$, $y=0$; the general case applies this to $f-y$)
Let $A=\{x\in[a,b]:f(x)<0\}$. It contains $a$ and is bounded above by $b$, so $c=\sup A$ exists by completeness.
If $f(c)>0$: sign preservation (Unit 4) gives $\delta>0$ with $f>0$ on $(c-\delta,c+\delta)$. Then no point of $A$ exceeds $c-\delta$, so $c-\delta$ is an upper bound smaller than $c$ — contradiction.
If $f(c)<0$: then $c<b$ (as $f(b)>0$), and $f<0$ on some $(c-\delta,c+\delta)\subseteq[a,b]$, putting points of $A$ above $c$ — contradicting "upper bound." So $f(c)=0$. ∎
Example Roots exist

Every $c>0$ has an $n$th root: $f(x)=x^n-c$ is continuous, $f(0)=-c<0$, and $f(1+c)\ge1+nc-c>0$ by Bernoulli's inequality (Unit 1), so the IVT gives a root in $(0,1+c)$.

Boundedness, then Extreme Value

Theorem Boundedness

A continuous function on $[a,b]$ is bounded there.

Proof idea
Let $A=\{x\in[a,b]:f\text{ is bounded on }[a,x]\}$; it contains $a$ and is bounded above by $b$, so $\alpha=\sup A$ exists.
Continuity at $\alpha$ bounds $f$ on a small interval around $\alpha$ (take $\varepsilon=1$). Splicing this onto $[a,x]$ for some $x\in A$ close to $\alpha$ shows: $\alpha\in A$, and if $\alpha<b$ then points beyond $\alpha$ would also lie in $A$ — impossible. So $\alpha=b$ and $f$ is bounded on all of $[a,b]$. ∎
Theorem Extreme Value (EVT)

A continuous function on $[a,b]$ attains a maximum and a minimum value.

Proof
By boundedness, $M=\sup\{f(x):x\in[a,b]\}$ exists. Suppose $f(x)<M$ for every $x$.
Then $g=\dfrac{1}{M-f}$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, hence bounded: $g\le K$ for some $K>0$. That rearranges to $f\le M-\tfrac1K$, making $M-\tfrac1K$ a smaller upper bound than the least one — contradiction. So the max is attained; apply this to $-f$ for the min. ∎
Both hypotheses are essential $1/x$ on $(0,1]$ is continuous but unbounded (interval not closed at $0$); $x$ on $(0,1)$ is bounded but attains neither max nor min; $\mathbb{R}$ itself is closed but not bounded, and $f(x)=x$ attains nothing. And a single jump discontinuity breaks the IVT.
Common pitfalls

• The interval must be closed and bounded for boundedness and the EVT.
• The IVT gives existence of $c$, never its value or uniqueness.
• "Attains" means some $c$ has $f(c)=M$ — a supremum alone is not a maximum.
• Continuity is needed on the whole interval; one bad point can break all three theorems.

Practice

Original problems written for this guide in the style of Spivak's exercises. Write a full solution before opening the answer.

Practice 1 · A root

Show $x^3-x-1=0$ has a real root.

Answer
$f(1)=-1<0$ and $f(2)=5>0$; by the IVT a root lies in $(1,2)$.
Practice 2 · Fixed point

Show any continuous $f:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ has a fixed point: $f(c)=c$.

Solution
Let $g(x)=f(x)-x$; then $g(0)=f(0)\ge0$ and $g(1)=f(1)-1\le0$. If either is $0$ we are done; otherwise the IVT gives $g(c)=0$.
Practice 3 · Counterexample

Why does the EVT fail for $f(x)=x$ on $(0,1)$?

Answer
The interval is open: the sup $1$ and inf $0$ are limits of values but are never attained. No contradiction with the EVT, whose hypothesis requires a closed interval.
Practice 4 · Odd degree

Prove every polynomial of odd degree has a real root.

Solution
Divide by the leading coefficient, so $p(x)=x^n+\cdots$ with $n$ odd. For $|x|$ large the leading term dominates: $p(x)>0$ for large $x>0$ and $p(x)<0$ for large negative $x$ (odd power). The IVT on a large interval gives a root.
Practice 5 · Rational values

Suppose $f$ is continuous on $[0,1]$ and takes only rational values. Prove $f$ is constant.

Solution
If $f(x_0)\ne f(x_1)$, some irrational $y$ lies between them, and the IVT forces $f$ to attain $y$ — contradicting "only rational values."
Practice 6 · Equal values half apart

Let $f$ be continuous on $[0,1]$ with $f(0)=f(1)$. Prove there is $c\in[0,\tfrac12]$ with $f(c)=f(c+\tfrac12)$.

Solution
Let $g(x)=f(x+\tfrac12)-f(x)$ on $[0,\tfrac12]$. Then $g(0)+g(\tfrac12)=f(1)-f(0)=0$, so $g$ takes values of opposite sign (or a zero) at the endpoints; the IVT gives $g(c)=0$.
Practice 7 · Two roots

Show $x^4-2x-5=0$ has at least two real roots.

Solution
$p(0)=-5<0$, $p(2)=16-4-5=7>0$, and $p(-2)=16+4-5=15>0$. The IVT gives one root in $(-2,0)$ and another in $(0,2)$.
Practice 8 · Where EVT is used

Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ with $f(x)>0$ for all $x$. Prove there is $m>0$ with $f(x)\ge m$ for every $x$ — and show this can fail on $(a,b)$.

Solution
By the EVT, $f$ attains a minimum $m=f(c)>0$, which works. On an open interval it fails: $f(x)=x$ on $(0,1)$ is positive with infimum $0$.
