Limits

The ε–δ definition, now as a tool you prove with, not just apply. In analysis every limit law is a theorem with a proof — and this is where you learn to build them.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

For two centuries calculus rested on infinitesimals nobody could define. The ε–δ limit answered "what does approaches mean?" and made the whole subject rigorous.

In the real world

Every instantaneous rate is a limit, and the convergence of every numerical algorithm is a limit. The ε–δ habit of "given a target tolerance, find the needed precision" is exactly how error analysis works.

Story & open questions

Berkeley mocked infinitesimals in 1734 as "the ghosts of departed quantities." Cauchy and Weierstrass made ε–δ rigorous a century later; Weierstrass then built a function continuous everywhere yet differentiable nowhere.

By the end you can
  • State the ε–δ definition precisely.
  • Prove limits directly from the definition.
  • Prove the limit laws.
  • Use the squeeze theorem.

The ε–δ definition

Definition Limit

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$ means: $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0$ such that $0<|x-a|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon$.

Example Quadratic

Prove $\lim_{x\to2}x^2=4$. Restrict $\delta\le1$ so $|x+2|<5$; then take $\delta=\min\{1,\varepsilon/5\}$ and $|x^2-4|=|x-2||x+2|<\varepsilon$.

Limit laws, proved

Theorem

If $\lim f=L$ and $\lim g=M$, then $\lim(f+g)=L+M$ and $\lim(fg)=LM$.

Proof idea of the sum law
Given $\varepsilon$, pick $\delta_1,\delta_2$ making each of $|f-L|,|g-M|<\varepsilon/2$.
For $\delta=\min$, $|(f+g)-(L+M)|\le|f-L|+|g-M|<\varepsilon$ by the triangle inequality. ∎

The squeeze theorem

Theorem

If $g\le f\le h$ near $a$ and $\lim g=\lim h=L$, then $\lim f=L$.

Example

$\lim_{x\to0}x^2\sin(1/x)=0$, since $-x^2\le x^2\sin(1/x)\le x^2$.

Common pitfalls

• $\delta$ may depend on $\varepsilon$ and $a$, never on $x$.
• Bound the "extra" factor by first restricting $\delta\le1$.
• The limit ignores the value $f(a)$ — that is the role of $0<|x-a|$.

Practice

Practice 1

Prove $\lim_{x\to1}(3x+2)=5$ from the definition.

Solution
$\delta=\varepsilon/3$: $|(3x+2)-5|=3|x-1|<\varepsilon$.
Practice 2

Prove the product law $\lim(fg)=LM$.

Hint
Write $fg-LM=f(g-M)+M(f-L)$; bound $f$ near $a$ and use both limits.
Practice 3

Find $\lim_{x\to0}x\cos(1/x)$ with justification.

Answer
$0$, by squeeze ($-|x|\le x\cos(1/x)\le|x|$).
