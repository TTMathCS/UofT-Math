Eigenvalues & Diagonalization

The finale: directions a map only stretches, not rotates. Finding them is how we choose the basis that makes a matrix diagonal — the most useful form there is. The theory continues into MAT247.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Eigenvectors are the special directions a linear map merely scales. In an eigenbasis the matrix is diagonal, so powers, exponentials, and long-term behaviour become trivial to compute.

In the real world

Eigenvalues run Google's PageRank, principal component analysis, the stability of dynamical systems and markets, the energy levels of quantum mechanics, and vibration modes in engineering.

Story & open questions

The "eigen" (German for "own") terminology comes from Hilbert. The Cayley–Hamilton theorem — every matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation — caps the course; when a matrix cannot be diagonalized, the Jordan form of MAT247 takes over.

By the end you can
  • Find eigenvalues from the characteristic polynomial.
  • Find eigenvectors and decide diagonalizability.
  • State the Cayley–Hamilton theorem.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Definition

$\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ with eigenvector $v\ne0$ if $Av=\lambda v$. The eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic polynomial $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$.

Example

For $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, the eigenvalues are $2$ and $3$ with eigenvectors $e_1,e_2$.

Diagonalization and Cayley–Hamilton

Theorem

$A$ is diagonalizable iff it has a basis of eigenvectors; then $A=PDP^{-1}$ with $D$ diagonal. An $n\times n$ matrix with $n$ distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable.

Theorem Cayley–Hamilton

Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial: $p_A(A)=0$.

Common pitfalls

• Eigenvectors must be nonzero by definition.
• Repeated eigenvalues may give too few independent eigenvectors — then the matrix is not diagonalizable.
• Over $\mathbb{R}$ a matrix may have no real eigenvalues (a rotation); over $\mathbb{C}$ its characteristic polynomial has $n$ roots counted with algebraic multiplicity, but it may still have fewer than $n$ independent eigenvectors.

Practice

Practice 1

Find the eigenvalues of $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

Answer
$2$ and $3$ (triangular — read the diagonal).
Practice 2

Is $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ diagonalizable?

Answer
No — only one independent eigenvector for the repeated eigenvalue $1$.
Practice 3

Verify Cayley–Hamilton for $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

Answer
$p_A(\lambda)=\lambda^2$, and indeed $A^2=0$.
