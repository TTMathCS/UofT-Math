Improper Integrals

Integrals over an infinite interval, or of a function that blows up, are defined as limits of ordinary integrals. The question is always the same: does that limit exist?

By the end you can
  • Evaluate type I (infinite interval) and type II (unbounded integrand) improper integrals.
  • Apply the $p$-test.
  • Use the comparison test for convergence.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Many of the most important integrals run to infinity or blow up somewhere — distributions over all of space, decaying signals, probabilities. Improper integrals define them carefully as limits.

In the real world

The Gaussian integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx=\sqrt{\pi}$ is what normalizes the bell curve. Laplace and Fourier transforms — over infinite domains — are the backbone of signal processing and control engineering. Expected values in probability are improper integrals.

Story & open questions

Torricelli's "Gabriel's Horn" (1640s) has finite volume but infinite surface area — you could fill it with paint but never paint it. The paradox shocked mathematicians and is a perfect demonstration that infinity does not behave the way intuition expects.

Type I — infinite intervals

Definition

$\displaystyle\int_a^\infty f=\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_a^t f$, when the limit exists (then the integral converges). Integrals over $(-\infty,\infty)$ must be split at a finite point; both pieces must converge.

Example

$\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\Big[-\frac1x \Big]_1^t=\lim_{t\to\infty}\Big(1-\frac1t\Big)=1.$

Type II — unbounded integrand

Definition

If $f$ blows up at $b$, $\displaystyle\int_a^b f=\lim_{t\to b^-}\int_a^t f$; if it blows up at $a$, use $\lim_{t\to a^+}\int_t^b f$. At an interior singularity, split the integral there; every piece must converge.

Example

$\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}}=\lim_{t\to0^+}\big[2\sqrt x\big]_t^1=2.$ Converges.

The $p$-test

Theorem

$\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^p}$ converges iff $p>1$.   $\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x^p}$ converges iff $p<1$.

Comparison test

Theorem

If $0\le f\le g$ near the same improper endpoint and $\int g$ converges, so does $\int f$. If $\int f$ diverges, so does $\int g$.

Example

$\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2+1}$ converges, since $\dfrac{1}{x^2+1}\le\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ and $\int_1^\infty x^{-2}$ converges.

Common pitfalls

• Always rewrite as a limit; don't plug $\infty$ in directly.
• Watch for hidden type-II behaviour (a singularity inside the interval).
• For the comparison test the functions must be nonnegative.

Practice

Problems adapted from the MAT137 question bank by Alfonso Gracia-Saz, used under CC BY-SA 4.0. Write full solutions before opening the answer.

Practice 1 · Evaluate

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\,dx$.

Answer
$\lim_{t\to\infty} [-e^{-x}]_0^t=1.$
Practice 2 · Divergence

Does $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x}$ converge?

Answer
No: $p=1$, and $\int_1^t x^{-1}\,dx=\ln t\to\infty$.
Practice 3 · Comparison

Determine convergence of $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{2+\sin x}{x^2} \,dx$.

Hint
$0\le\dfrac{2+\sin x}{x^2} \le\dfrac{3}{x^2}$; converges by comparison.
Practice 4 · Evaluate

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2+x}$.

Solution
$\dfrac1{x^2+x}=\dfrac1x -\dfrac1{x+1}$, so $\int_1^t=\left[\ln\dfrac{x}{x+1}\right]_1^t\to0-\ln\tfrac12 =\ln2.$
Practice 5 · p-test

For which $p$ do $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^p}$ and $\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x^p}$ converge?

Answer
$\int_1^\infty$ converges iff $p>1$; $\int_0^1$ converges iff $p<1$. (The trouble is at $\infty$ in the first and at $0$ in the second.)
Practice 6 · A subtle one

What is $\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x}$?

Answer
It diverges: it is improper at $0$, and $\int_0^1 dx/x$ already diverges, so the symmetry "$=0$" argument is invalid.
Practice 7 · Comparison

Determine convergence of $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x+e^x}$.

Answer
$0<\dfrac1{x+e^x} <e^{-x}$ and $\int_1^\infty e^{-x}\,dx$ converges, so it converges.
Practice 8 · Compare two

Which converge: $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{1+\cos^2x}{x^{2/3}}\,dx$ or $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{1+\cos^2x}{x^{4/3}}\,dx$?

Answer
The numerator lies in $[1,2]$. The first diverges ($p=\tfrac23\le1$, bound below by $1/x^{2/3}$); the second converges ($p=\tfrac43>1$, bound above by $2/x^{4/3}$).
