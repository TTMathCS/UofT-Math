Prologue · The Story & Reach of Calculus

Why Calculus Exists

Calculus is the mathematics of change and of the infinite. It took humanity roughly two thousand years to build, cost its inventors a bitter feud, and was attacked as logically bankrupt for over a century before being made airtight. This is the story of why it exists — and why this course rebuilds it, from proofs up. It closes with a tour of where calculus runs in the world today.

The two questions behind everything
The tangent problem

How fast is something changing at a single instant — the speed of a falling stone exactly now, not its average over a second? This is the derivative.

The area problem

What is the area, volume, or total accumulated by a quantity whose boundary or rate keeps curving and changing? This is the integral.

The shock

Calculus is the discovery that these two questions — slope and area, the instantaneous and the accumulated — are secretly the same question, inverse to each other. That link is the Fundamental Theorem (Unit 8).

An idea older than algebra

The ancients could measure straight edges and static shapes. Motion and curves defeated them. As early as ~450 BC, Zeno of Elea posed paradoxes — Achilles can never overtake the tortoise, an arrow in flight is motionless at every instant — that made one thing clear: anyone who wanted to talk about the infinitely small, or about adding infinitely many pieces, was walking on thin ice. The warning would echo for two millennia.

Archimedes almost had it (c. 250 BC)

Building on Eudoxus, Archimedes perfected the method of exhaustion: trap a curved region between inscribed and circumscribed polygons, then make the gap shrink until the area is pinned exactly. With it he proved the area of a parabolic segment is exactly $\tfrac43$ of an inscribed triangle, trapped $\pi$ between $\tfrac{223}{71}$ and $\tfrac{22}{7}$ (about $3.1408$ and $3.1429$), and found the volume of a sphere.

Why start with a hexagon? The inscribed regular hexagon is the one polygon whose side equals the circle's radius exactly — so for a circle of diameter $1$ its perimeter is exactly $3$, with no square root at all, giving the very first bound $\pi>3$. The circumscribed hexagon gives $2\sqrt3$. From there Archimedes simply doubled: $6\to12\to24\to48\to96$. (Starting from a square would force a $\sqrt2$ immediately, with no clean opening value.)
The method of exhaustion: squeezing π

A circle of diameter $1$ has circumference exactly $\pi$. Archimedes trapped it between the perimeters of an inscribed polygon (a little too short) and a circumscribed one (a little too long), then doubled the sides — $6, 12, 24, 48, 96$ — using only square roots and arithmetic, no sine or tangent tables. Press Double the sides and watch $\pi$ emerge. This is the direct ancestor of the limit you meet in Unit 2.

Lost and found

In a work called The Method — lost for centuries, rediscovered on a scraped-over manuscript in 1906 — Archimedes revealed he first found these results by mentally slicing shapes into infinitely many strips and balancing them on a lever. That is integration. But he distrusted it and never accepted it as proof. Related ideas continued to develop in China, India, the Islamic world, and Europe before a systematic calculus emerged in the seventeenth century.

You will meet this again His squeeze is the direct ancestor of the limit (Unit 2) and his strips are the Riemann sums that define the integral (Unit 7).

The scientific revolution makes it urgent (1600s)

When Europe turned to measuring the heavens and the motion of bodies, the old geometry buckled and the pieces of calculus appeared all at once:

  • Kepler (1609–1619) needed areas to state his planetary laws — a planet sweeps equal areas in equal times — and sliced volumes into infinitesimal layers to gauge wine barrels.
  • Galileo (1638) found falling bodies travel a distance proportional to time squared, which made "velocity at an instant" a question that demanded a new tool.
  • Descartes and Fermat (1630s) fused algebra with geometry: a curve became an equation. Fermat could then find tangents and locate maxima and minima.
  • Cavalieri summed "indivisibles," and Isaac Barrow — Newton's own teacher — glimpsed that the tangent and area problems are inverses.

These and many other contributions put the main pieces on the table. Newton and Leibniz then developed systematic versions of calculus independently.

Newton and Leibniz, independently (1665–1684)

Isaac Newton, sent home during the plague years of 1665–66, developed his "method of fluxions" alongside his work in physics. In the Principia (1687), he presented largely geometric arguments deriving Kepler's laws from universal gravitation. The underlying ideas were deeply tied to his calculus.

Independently, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz built the same calculus from a different direction and gave us the language we still write today: the differential $dx$, the ratio $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$, and the integral sign $\displaystyle\int$ — an elongated S for "sum."

The feud

A bitter priority dispute erupted over who invented calculus first. It poisoned relations between British and Continental mathematicians for decades and helped isolate parts of British mathematics from Continental developments. Leibniz's differential and integral notation is the one used in this course.

The crisis: "ghosts of departed quantities" (1734)

Calculus worked remarkably well, but its early infinitesimal arguments often lacked a precise foundation. An infinitesimal could be treated as nonzero during a calculation and then discarded as negligible. In 1734 Bishop George Berkeley ridiculed these quantities as "the ghosts of departed quantities." His criticism exposed a genuine gap between the successful computations and their justification.

The rescue came in the 1800s. Bolzano, Cauchy, and Weierstrass replaced the vague infinitesimal with the precise limit — the $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ definition. Riemann made the integral exact, and Dedekind and Cantor built the real numbers themselves. Calculus became analysis: rigorous, proven, certain.

This is the road you are about to walk MAT137 retraces the rescue. Unit 1 is logic and proof; Unit 2 is the $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ limit that ended the crisis. When the work feels hard, remember: you are rebuilding in one term what took the best minds in Europe two centuries to get right.

How calculus built the modern world

Once it could be trusted, calculus became the native language of every quantitative science. A short arc through modernization:

  • 1700s. Euler and the Bernoullis turned calculus into a vast engine; Lagrange and Laplace modelled the solar system and helped found probability.
  • 1800s. Fourier analyzed the flow of heat and gave us Fourier series; Maxwell compressed all of electricity, magnetism, and light into four calculus equations — making radio, power grids, and electronics possible. The steam engine powered the Industrial Revolution, and the thermodynamics built to understand it is written in calculus.
  • 1900s. Relativity and quantum mechanics are written in calculus; control theory guided Apollo to the Moon; signal processing built global telecommunications.
  • Today. The derivative is how machines learn, and the integral is how we measure probability and risk. (The final section, Calculus at Work, is devoted to this.)

So why prove it? Why MAT137?

You can compute derivatives without ever proving a theorem. But the history is a warning written in fire: human intuition about the infinite is unreliable — it was wrong for two hundred years. This course teaches you both to compute and to know exactly why the computation is valid. That is the difference between using a tool and being able to trust it — between a technician and a scientist, engineer, or mathematician whose conclusions can be believed.

A small challenge

Read this prologue once now, and once again on the last day of the course. The second time, you will recognize every name — Archimedes' squeeze, Cauchy's limit, Newton's theorem — because you will have proved their ideas yourself.

Calculus at work: where it all goes

Three and a half centuries after Newton, calculus is not a museum piece — it runs quietly underneath most of modern technology. Here is where the ideas from the 14 units actually show up at work today, with the unit each one traces back to. If the proofs ever feel abstract, this is what they are for.

DERIVATIVES

AI & machine learning

Most modern neural networks — image recognizers and language models alike — are trained with gradient-based optimization: take derivatives of a loss function with respect to many parameters and update them. Backpropagation applies the chain rule (Unit 3) efficiently at enormous scale.

PDEs · OPTIMA

Finance & risk

The Black–Scholes equation that prices options is a differential equation; the "Greeks" traders watch are literally derivatives of price (Unit 3). Continuous compounding is $e^{rt}$ (Unit 4), portfolio choice is constrained optimization (Unit 6), and expected payoff is an integral over probability (Unit 7).

INTEGRALS

Engineering & control

Autopilots, thermostats, and robot arms run on feedback control — the "I" and "D" of a PID controller are an integral and a derivative. Signal processing rests on Fourier and Laplace transforms (integrals, Units 7–9), and aerodynamics solves the Navier–Stokes equations by numerical integration.

RATES

Medicine & biology

Drug dosing is modelled by differential equations for concentration over time; the epidemic curves of 2020 came from SIR models — systems of derivatives. CT and MRI scanners rebuild images by inverting integrals. "Flattening the curve" is a statement about a derivative.

CURVES

Graphics, games & film

The smooth shapes of fonts and car bodies are Bézier curves and splines; physics engines integrate the equations of motion every frame; photorealistic rendering solves the "rendering equation," an integral over all incoming light (Unit 7). Animation is calculus at 60 frames a second.

ODEs

Space, GPS & navigation

Rocket trajectories and orbits are differential equations and optimization; GPS works only because it corrects for the time dilation of relativity and computes position from rates of change of signals. The Apollo guidance computer ran on numerical calculus.

INTEGRALS

Data science & statistics

A probability is an area under a density curve — an integral (Units 7, 12). Maximum-likelihood and Bayesian methods fit models by setting derivatives to zero (Unit 6). Much of statistics is calculus in disguise, which is exactly why a rigorous calculus background pays off in a data career.

SERIES

Climate, energy & operations

Climate models discretize partial differential equations across the whole atmosphere; power grids, supply chains, and logistics are vast optimization problems (Unit 6). The series and convergence of Units 11 and 13–14 underlie the numerical methods that make all of it computable.

How calculus helps computers approximate functions

Computers represent real numbers with finite precision, so values such as $\sin(1)$ and $e$ must be approximated. Taylor series (Unit 14) provide one principled route; practical math libraries also use range reduction, carefully chosen polynomial or rational approximations, tables, and other algorithms. Calculus supplies both approximations and ways to bound their errors.

Example Approximating $e$

$e=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac1{n!}=1+1+\tfrac12+\tfrac16+\tfrac1{24} +\cdots$. The first seven terms, through $1/6!$, give $2.71806\ldots$. The factorial denominator makes the truncation error shrink rapidly, and Taylor's theorem makes that error quantifiable.

The thread back to these units

A quick map of where each big idea returns as a tool:

  • The gradient that trains AI is the derivative of Unit 3.
  • Continuous growth, decay, and compounding are the exponential and logarithm of Unit 4.
  • Every "best" design — cheapest, fastest, strongest — is the optimization of Units 5 and 6.
  • Continuous probability calculations, drug doses, rendered images, and accumulated totals use integrals from Units 7–10.
  • The infinite sums behind many numerical approximations are the sequences and series of Units 11–14.
The real payoff Learn the 14 units well and you are not memorizing rules — you are learning the operating system of modern quantitative work. The same ideas you prove on paper this term support much of the technology you will build.
