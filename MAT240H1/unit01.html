Fields & Vector Spaces

Linear algebra starts not with matrices but with axioms: a field of scalars and a vector space over it. Everything later — bases, dimension, eigenvalues — is a consequence of these rules, which is why the first proofs in the course are about the axioms themselves.

By the end you can
  • State the field axioms and work in $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, and $\mathbb{Z}_p$.
  • State the eight vector space axioms.
  • Prove the first consequences: $0v=0$, $(-1)v=-v$, cancellation.
  • Verify whether a given set with given operations is a vector space.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Defining a vector space abstractly — by axioms, not as arrows or columns — means one theorem covers polynomials, functions, and data vectors at once. Abstraction buys reach.

In the real world

Vector spaces over $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$ are the language of machine learning (data as vectors), quantum mechanics, and signal processing; finite fields power error-correcting codes and cryptography.

Story & open questions

Grassmann and Peano formalized vector spaces in the late 1800s; the abstract axiomatic view took hold in the 20th century and is now the default language of pure and applied mathematics alike.

Fields

Definition Field

A field $F$ is a set with two operations $+$ and $\cdot$ such that both are associative and commutative, distributivity holds, there are identities $0\ne1$, every element has an additive inverse, and every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. Examples: $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, and the integers mod $p$, written $\mathbb{Z}_p$, for prime $p$.

Example Why $p$ must be prime
In $\mathbb{Z}_6$: $2\cdot3=6\equiv0$, yet $2\ne0$ and $3\ne0$. If $2$ had an inverse $u$, then $3=(u\cdot2)\cdot3=u\cdot0=0$ — false. So $\mathbb{Z}_6$ is not a field.
For prime $p$, every $a\not\equiv0$ has an inverse — e.g. in $\mathbb{Z}_5$, $3^{-1}=2$ because $3\cdot2=6\equiv1$. ∎
Why a field and not something weaker? Linear algebra constantly divides by scalars — to normalize a vector, to solve $ax=b$, to run row reduction. Division by every nonzero scalar is exactly what the field axioms guarantee and what $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Z}_6$ lack.

Vector spaces

Definition Vector space

A vector space over a field $F$ is a set $V$ with vector addition and scalar multiplication satisfying, for all $u,v,w\in V$ and $a,b\in F$: (VS1) $u+v=v+u$; (VS2) $(u+v)+w=u+(v+w)$; (VS3) a zero vector with $v+0=v$; (VS4) additive inverses; (VS5) $1v=v$; (VS6) $(ab)v=a(bv)$; (VS7) $a(u+v)=au+av$; (VS8) $(a+b)v=av+bv$.

Examples

$F^n$ (coordinate tuples), the $m\times n$ matrices $M_{m\times n}(F)$, the polynomials $P(F)$, and the functions from any set $S$ into $F$ — with pointwise operations — are all vector spaces over $F$. So is $\mathbb{C}$ as a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$: the field of scalars is part of the data.

First proofs from the axioms

Theorem Cancellation

If $u+w=v+w$ in a vector space, then $u=v$.

Proof
Add the inverse $-w$ (VS4) to both sides and use associativity (VS2): $u=(u+w)+(-w)=(v+w)+(-w)=v$. ∎ (Consequence: the zero vector and inverses are unique.)
Theorem Basic identities

For every $v\in V$ and $a\in F$: $0v=0$,   $a\,0=0$,   $(-1)v=-v$,   and $av=0$ implies $a=0$ or $v=0$.

Proof of two of them
$0v=(0+0)v=0v+0v$ by VS8; cancel $0v$ to get $0v=0$. ∎
$v+(-1)v=1v+(-1)v=(1+(-1))v=0v=0$, so $(-1)v$ is the additive inverse of $v$. ∎
If $av=0$ with $a\ne0$: $v=1v=(a^{-1}a)v=a^{-1}(av) =a^{-1}0=0$ — this is where "every nonzero scalar is invertible" earns its keep. ∎

How to verify (or refute) a vector space

Routine
First check the two closures: is $u+v$ in the set? is $av$ in the set? Most non-examples already fail here.
Locate the zero vector and check it belongs to the set.
Only then walk the eight axioms — for subsets of a known vector space with the inherited operations, the axioms are automatic and closure is the whole question (Unit 2).
Common pitfalls

• Closure under both operations must be checked first — it is not an axiom you get for free.
• The zero vector must actually lie in the set; a set missing $0$ is disqualified immediately.
• Scalars come from the field: over $\mathbb{Z}_p$ arithmetic wraps around, and facts like "$2\ne0$" can fail (in $\mathbb{Z}_2$, $2=0$).
• $0v=0$ and $(-1)v=-v$ are theorems, not axioms — a proof may not assume them before proving them.

Practice

Original problems written for this guide in the style of Friedberg, Insel & Spence exercises. Write a full solution before opening the answer.

Practice 1 · Subset check

Is $\{(x,y):x+y=0\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^2$, with the usual operations, a vector space?

Answer
Yes. It contains $(0,0)$, and sums and scalar multiples of solutions of $x+y=0$ still satisfy it.
Practice 2 · Subset check

Is $\{(x,y):x+y=1\}$ a vector space?

Answer
No — it does not contain $(0,0)$, and it is not closed: the sum of two solutions has $x+y=2$.
Practice 3 · Modular arithmetic

Compute $3\cdot4$ and $3+4$ in $\mathbb{Z}_5$.

Answer
$12\equiv2$ and $7\equiv2\pmod 5$ — both equal $2$.
Practice 4 · From the axioms

Prove from the axioms that $0v=0$ for every vector $v$.

Solution
$0v=(0+0)v=0v+0v$ by VS8; adding $-(0v)$ to both sides (cancellation) leaves $0=0v$.
Practice 5 · From the axioms

Prove $(-1)v=-v$, i.e. that $(-1)v$ is the additive inverse of $v$.

Solution
$v+(-1)v=(1+(-1))v=0v=0$ using VS5, VS8, and Practice 4; uniqueness of inverses (cancellation) finishes it.
Practice 6 · Inverses mod p

Find $4^{-1}$ in $\mathbb{Z}_7$.

Answer
$4\cdot2=8\equiv1 \pmod 7$, so $4^{-1}=2$.
Practice 7 · Not a field

Show that $\mathbb{Z}_6$ is not a field.

Solution
$2\cdot3\equiv0$ with $2,3\ne0$. A field has no such zero divisors: if $2^{-1}$ existed, then $3=2^{-1}(2\cdot3)=0$, a contradiction.
Practice 8 · The field matters

Explain why $\mathbb{R}$ is a vector space over $\mathbb{Q}$, and why $\mathbb{Q}$ is not a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$ with the usual operations.

Answer
Real numbers can be added and multiplied by rational scalars, and all eight axioms hold. But $\mathbb{Q}$ is not closed under multiplication by real scalars: $\sqrt2\cdot1\notin\mathbb{Q}$.
