Power Series

Functions as infinite polynomials, now on rigorous footing: radius of convergence, and — thanks to uniform convergence on compact subintervals — term-by-term calculus inside that radius. The capstone of the course.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

A power series turns a complicated function into something computable, differentiable, and integrable term by term — with the radius of convergence telling you exactly where that is valid.

In the real world

It is how computers evaluate transcendental functions, how differential equations are solved by series methods, and how machine learning and applied mathematics use truncated expansions to approximate and analyze functions.

Story & open questions

Madhava found power series for sine, cosine, and arctangent around 1400, well before Newton or Leibniz. From these same ideas comes Euler's identity $e^{i\pi}+1=0$, often called the most beautiful equation in mathematics — a single power series ($e^x$) evaluated at an imaginary argument.

By the end you can
  • Find the radius and interval of convergence, including testing the endpoints separately.
  • Differentiate and integrate power series term by term, and justify why this is valid.
  • Recognize and use the common Maclaurin series.
  • Explain why a power series's coefficients are uniquely determined by the function it represents.

Radius of convergence

Definition

A power series $\sum c_n(x-a)^n$ converges absolutely for $|x-a|<R$ and diverges for $|x-a|>R$, for some $R\in[0,\infty]$ called the radius of convergence; convergence at the two endpoints $x=a\pm R$ must be checked separately.

Cauchy–Hadamard formula

$\dfrac1R=\operatorname{limsup}_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{|c_n|}$ (or, when the limit exists, $\dfrac1R=\lim\left|\dfrac{c_{n+1}}{c_n}\right|$ by the ratio test) — this is exactly the root/ratio test from Unit 5, applied with $x$ fixed and solving the resulting inequality $L<1$ for $|x-a|$.

Example Endpoint behaviour

$\sum\dfrac{x^n}{n}$ has $R=1$ by the ratio test. At $x=1$: $\sum1/n$ diverges. At $x=-1$: $\sum(-1)^n/n$ converges (alternating test). So the interval of convergence is $[-1,1)$ — asymmetric, because the two endpoints genuinely behave differently.

Term-by-term calculus

Theorem

Within radius $R$, a power series converges uniformly on every closed subinterval $[a-r,a+r]$ with $r<R$ (by the M-test, comparing with a convergent geometric-type series), so it may be differentiated and integrated term by term there, with the differentiated/integrated series having the same radius $R$.

Example

Integrating $\dfrac{1}{1+x}=\sum_{n\ge0}(-x)^n$ term by term gives $\ln(1+x)=\sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{(-1)^n x^{n+1}}{n+1}$ for $|x|<1$.

Example Differentiating

Differentiating $\dfrac1{1-x}=\sum x^n$ term by term gives $\dfrac1{(1-x)^2}=\sum_{n\ge1}nx^{n-1}$, valid for $|x|<1$.

Uniqueness of the coefficients If a power series represents $f$ near $a$, its coefficients must be $c_n=f^{(n)}(a)/n!$ — differentiating term by term $n$ times and evaluating at $x=a$ kills every term except the $n$-th. So a function has at most one power series representation about a given centre: it must be its own Taylor series.

Common Maclaurin series

Reference list

$e^x=\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{x^n}{n!}$ ($R=\infty$); $\sin x=\sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{(-1)^nx^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}$ ($R=\infty$); $\cos x=\sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{(-1)^nx^{2n}}{(2n)!}$ ($R=\infty$); $\dfrac1{1-x}=\sum_{n\ge0}x^n$ ($R=1$); $\ln(1+x)=\sum_{n\ge1}\dfrac{(-1)^{n+1}x^n}{n}$ ($R=1$); $\arctan x=\sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{(-1)^nx^{2n+1}}{2n+1}$ ($R=1$).

Example Building a new series

$e^{-x^2}=\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{(-x^2)^n}{n!}= \sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{(-1)^nx^{2n}}{n!}$ — substitute directly into the known series for $e^x$, valid for all $x$ since the original has $R=\infty$.

Common pitfalls

• Endpoints need separate testing with the Unit 5 series tests; the radius alone never decides them.
• Term-by-term differentiation and integration are valid strictly inside the radius — the differentiated series can behave differently right at the boundary.
• A function need not equal its Taylor series everywhere the series converges (recall Cauchy's $e^{-1/x^2}$ example from Unit 4) — but if it does, the representation is unique.
• Substituting into a known series (as with $e^{-x^2}$) changes the radius according to the substitution — always recheck it.

Practice

Practice 1

Radius of convergence of $\sum\dfrac{x^n}{n!}$?

Answer
$R=\infty$ (ratio test: $\left|\tfrac{x}{n+1}\right|\to0$ for every $x$).
Practice 2

Maclaurin series of $e^{-x^2}$?

Answer
$\sum\dfrac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{n!}$, $R=\infty$.
Practice 3

Interval of convergence of $\sum\dfrac{(x-2)^n}{n\,3^n}$?

Solution
Ratio test gives $R=3$, centred at $2$: candidate interval $(-1,5)$. At $x=5$: $\sum1/n$ diverges. At $x=-1$: $\sum(-1)^n/n$ converges. So $[-1,5)$.
Practice 4 · Cauchy–Hadamard

Find $R$ for $\sum n^n x^n$.

Answer
$\sqrt[n]{|c_n|}=n\to\infty$, so $1/R=\infty$, giving $R=0$ — the series converges only at $x=0$.
Practice 5 · Term-by-term integration

Use $\arctan x=\sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{(-1)^nx^{2n+1}}{2n+1}$ at $x=1$ to justify $\dfrac{\pi}{4}=1-\dfrac13+\dfrac15-\cdots$ (you may assume the series' behaviour at the endpoint without proving it).

Answer
Substituting $x=1$ into the arctangent series gives $\arctan(1)=\pi/4$ on the left and the stated alternating series on the right — a classical series for $\pi$.
Practice 6 · Differentiating twice

Starting from $\dfrac1{1-x}=\sum_{n\ge0}x^n$, find a series for $\dfrac{2}{(1-x)^3}$.

Solution
Differentiate twice: $\dfrac{2}{(1-x)^3}=\sum_{n\ge2}n(n-1)x^{n-2}$, for $|x|<1$.
Practice 7 · Uniqueness

Two power series centred at $0$ both equal $\cos x$ on $(-1,1)$. What can you conclude about their coefficients, and why?

Answer
They must be identical, term by term — coefficients of a power series representation are forced to be $f^{(n)}(0)/n!$ by repeated term-by-term differentiation, so there is only one power series for $\cos x$ at a given centre.
Practice 8 · Radius after substitution

$\dfrac1{1-x}=\sum x^n$ has $R=1$. What is the radius of convergence of $\dfrac1{1-x^3}$ as a power series in $x$?

Answer
Substitute $x^3$ for $x$: $\sum(x^3)^n=\sum x^{3n}$, which needs $|x^3|<1$, i.e. $|x|<1$ — the radius is still $R=1$.
