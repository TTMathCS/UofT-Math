Prose I: Narration & Point of View

Every story is told by someone, from somewhere, with access to some things and not others. Those constraints are chosen, and they shape what the reader can know — which makes them the first thing to analyse in any piece of fiction.

By the end you can
  • Identify the narrative person and its consequences.
  • Distinguish narrator from focaliser.
  • Recognise free indirect discourse.
  • Argue that a narrator is unreliable, with evidence.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

What a reader is allowed to know is the novelist's main lever. Withhold a character's thoughts and they become opaque; grant them and the reader cannot judge from outside. Every plot effect depends on these settings.

In the real world

News reporting makes the same choices — whose perspective frames the event, whose motives are explained, whose are left unexplained. The analysis transfers directly.

Story & open questions

Wayne Booth named the unreliable narrator in 1961, though the technique is far older. The interesting question is how a reader detects unreliability at all, given that the text's only voice is the one being doubted.

Person

Definition
First person. "I". The narrator is a character. Immediate and limited — and always potentially self-serving.
Second person. "You". Rare, and never neutral. It either implicates the reader or marks a speaker addressing themselves.
Third person. "He", "she", "they". The narrator stands outside the story.
Rule Third person has settings
Omniscient: access to any character's thoughts, and to information no character has.
Limited: access to one character's mind only. Everyone else is observed from outside.
Objective: no minds at all. Only what a camera could record.

The setting can change between chapters, and where it changes is usually deliberate. A novel that grants access to one character and withholds it from another has made a judgement about them.

Example What the choice costs

Consider a betrayal told three ways.

First person, by the betrayed: the reader shares the shock but cannot know the other's reasons. Sympathy is easy, judgement impossible.
Omniscient: both sets of reasons available. The reader may sympathise with both and blame neither, which is a different book entirely.
Objective: only actions and dialogue. The reader must infer everything, and the withholding itself becomes oppressive.

Same events, three different novels. That is why point of view is the first thing to establish.

Narrator versus focaliser

Definition
The narrator is who speaks.
The focaliser is who sees.

They are often the same, but not always — and the gap is one of the most useful things to notice.

Example Where they separate

An adult narrating their childhood. The narrator is the adult, with adult vocabulary and hindsight. The focaliser may be the child, who did not understand what was happening.

The reader gets the child's limited perception described in the adult's knowing language, so the gap between them is the meaning — everything the child missed is visible in how the adult phrases it.
A text can also narrate in third person while focalising tightly through one character, so the prose knows only what that character knows even though the voice is external.
Definition

Free indirect discourse blends the narrator's voice with a character's thoughts, without quotation marks or "she thought".

Direct: She thought, "This is intolerable."
Indirect: She thought that it was intolerable.
Free indirect: This was intolerable.

The third has no attribution at all, so the reader cannot cleanly separate the character's judgement from the narration's. That ambiguity is exactly what the technique is for.

Free indirect discourse enables irony

Because the narration adopts a character's terms without endorsing them, the reader can be invited to see past the character while inhabiting their view. If a passage states something absurd in a character's own idiom with no comment, the absence of comment is the comment.

Unreliable narration

Definition

An unreliable narrator gives an account the text itself invites you to doubt. The doubt must come from the text, not from disliking them.

Rule Evidence for unreliability
Internal contradiction. Two accounts of the same event that do not match.
Detail against interpretation. The narrator reports something that undercuts their own reading of it.
Other characters' reactions. People respond as though something different happened.
Overinsistence. Repeated protestation where none was called for.

Any of these is demonstrable. "The narrator seems dishonest" is not.

Example Building the case

An invented passage: "I explained calmly, as I always do. She left the room before I had finished. I sat for some time afterwards, and my hands were steady."

"Calmly, as I always do" is overinsistent — nobody volunteers this unless it is in question.
Her leaving mid-sentence contradicts the account of a calm explanation.
"My hands were steady" answers a charge that was never made, which tells the reader what the narrator fears is visible.
Three pieces of textual evidence, none of which requires guessing at the narrator's psychology. That is what an unreliability argument looks like.
Common pitfalls

• Assuming first person means unreliable. It only means limited.
• Confusing narrator with author.
• Missing free indirect discourse and attributing a character's judgement to the narration.
• Claiming unreliability from dislike rather than evidence.
• Ignoring where the point of view shifts. Shifts are always deliberate.

Practice

Work each fully before opening the answer. These use invented passages so the method is visible without depending on a set text.

Practice 1 · Identify

"She did not know he had already gone." What narration is this?

Answer
Third person with access beyond her knowledge — omniscient, or at least not limited to her. A strictly limited narration could not report what she did not know.
Practice 2 · Free indirect

Rewrite "He thought the meeting was pointless" as free indirect discourse.

Answer
"The meeting was pointless." The attribution disappears and the judgement sits in the narration.
Practice 3 · The effect

What does that rewrite change?

Answer
The reader can no longer cleanly separate his opinion from the narration's, so they inhabit his view rather than observing it — and the text can now be ironic at his expense without saying so.
Practice 4 · Narrator and focaliser

Give a case where they differ.

Answer
An adult recounting childhood: adult voice, child's perception. The gap between what was seen and how it is now described carries the meaning.
Practice 5 · Evidence

A narrator says "I have never lied to her." What might that signal?

Answer
Overinsistence — the denial raises a question nobody asked. On its own it proves nothing; with other evidence it contributes to a case.
Practice 6 · Objective narration

What is the effect of narration with no access to any mind?

Solution
The reader must infer everything from action and speech, which can feel clinical or claustrophobic. It also puts every character at the same distance, so no one can be privileged by sympathy — which may be exactly the point.
Practice 7 · A shift

A novel is limited third person until chapter ten, then becomes omniscient. What might you argue?

Answer
That the reader's confinement to one mind was doing work which is now over — perhaps the character's isolation has ended, or the novel is revealing what that confinement concealed. Argue from what the new access shows.
Practice 8 · Second person

Why is second-person narration never neutral?

Answer
"You" either casts the reader as the protagonist, which forces complicity, or marks a speaker addressing themselves, which implies dissociation. Either way it draws attention to itself.

Deep practice extensions

Use these after the main practice set. They test whether the distinctions have actually landed.

Extension 1 · Speaks versus sees

What is the difference between narrator and focaliser?

Guide
The narrator speaks; the focaliser perceives. They often coincide, and the gap when they do not is analytically rich.
Extension 2 · Detecting FID

How do you recognise free indirect discourse?

Guide
A character's judgement appears in the narration with no "she thought" and no quotation marks.
Extension 3 · Unreliability

What makes an unreliability claim arguable?

Guide
Textual evidence: contradiction, detail against interpretation, others' reactions, or overinsistence.
Extension 4 · First person

Does first person imply unreliability?

Guide
No. It implies limitation. Unreliability is an additional claim requiring its own evidence.
