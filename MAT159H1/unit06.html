Uniform Convergence

The capstone idea of the course: a stronger kind of convergence for sequences of functions, strong enough to let you swap limits with integrals and derivatives.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Pointwise convergence is too weak: a limit of continuous functions can be discontinuous, and you cannot freely swap limits with integrals. Uniform convergence is the condition that fixes this.

In the real world

It justifies term-by-term differentiation and integration of series — the silent step behind Fourier analysis, differential-equation solutions, and much of applied mathematics.

Story & open questions

Early 19th-century mathematicians made famous errors by assuming limits could always be swapped. Weierstrass isolated uniform convergence as the missing hypothesis — a milestone in the rigorisation of analysis.

By the end you can
  • Distinguish pointwise from uniform convergence.
  • Use the sup-norm test for uniform convergence.
  • State when limits, integrals, and derivatives may be exchanged.

Two kinds of convergence

Definition

$f_n\to f$ uniformly on $S$ if $\sup_{x\in S}|f_n(x)-f(x)|\to0$. The single $N$ works for all $x$ at once — that is the difference from pointwise.

Example

$f_n(x)=x^n$ on $[0,1]$ converges pointwise to a discontinuous limit (0 on $[0,1)$, 1 at $1$), so the convergence is not uniform.

Why it matters

Theorem

If $f_n\to f$ uniformly and each $f_n$ is continuous, then $f$ is continuous. On a closed bounded interval, uniform convergence of integrable functions permits $\int\lim=\lim\int$. For derivatives, one standard theorem also requires $f_n'$ to converge uniformly and $f_n(x_0)$ to converge at one point; then the limit is differentiable with derivative $\lim f_n'$.

Common pitfalls

• Pointwise convergence does not preserve continuity or allow swapping limits.
• Check the $\sup$ of the difference, not just each point.
• Uniform convergence of $f_n$ does not by itself give convergence of $f_n'$.

Practice

Practice 1

Show $f_n(x)=x/n$ converges uniformly to $0$ on $[0,1]$.

Answer
$\sup|x/n|=1/n\to0$.
Practice 2

Show $f_n(x)=x^n$ is not uniform on $[0,1]$.

Hint
$\sup|x^n-f|=1$ near $x=1$ for every $n$.
Practice 3

Does $f_n(x)=\dfrac{\sin(nx)}{n}$ converge uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$?

Answer
Yes; $\sup\le1/n\to0$.
