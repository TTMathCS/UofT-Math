The Spectral Theorem

The high point of the course. Over $\mathbb{C}$, normal is exactly the right hypothesis: a normal operator has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, full stop. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the same is true for symmetric operators. Every diagonalization failure from MAT240H1 was a failure of this hypothesis.

By the end you can
  • State both versions and say exactly which field each needs.
  • Prove the complex case via Schur's theorem.
  • Orthogonally diagonalize a symmetric matrix by hand.
  • Write the spectral resolution $T=\sum\lambda_iP_i$ and use it.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

MAT240H1 could diagonalize when it got lucky. This theorem replaces luck with a checkable hypothesis, and pays a bonus: the eigenbasis is orthonormal, so the change-of-basis matrix is unitary and its inverse is free.

In the real world

Principal component analysis is the spectral theorem applied to a covariance matrix. The normal modes of a vibrating structure are its eigenvectors. In quantum mechanics the theorem is the reason an observable has a complete set of states with real measured values.

Story & open questions

Cauchy proved the real symmetric case in 1829 while studying quadratic surfaces. Hilbert coined "spectrum" around 1904 for the infinite-dimensional analogue — before quantum mechanics gave the word its physical meaning, which he later called a lucky accident.

Schur's theorem

Theorem Schur

Let $T$ be an operator on a finite-dimensional complex inner product space. Then there is an orthonormal basis in which $[T]$ is upper triangular. Equivalently, every complex square matrix is unitarily equivalent to an upper triangular one: $A=UTU^*$ with $U$ unitary.

Proof Induction on dimension
Over $\mathbb{C}$ the characteristic polynomial of $T^*$ has a root, so $T^*$ has an eigenvector: $T^*w=\mu w$ with $\|w\|=1$.
Let $W=\operatorname{span}\{w\}$ and consider $W^\perp$, of dimension $n-1$. It is $T$-invariant: for $v\in W^\perp$, $\langle Tv,w\rangle=\langle v,T^*w\rangle=\langle v,\mu w\rangle =\overline{\mu}\langle v,w\rangle=0$.
By induction $T|_{W^\perp}$ has an orthonormal basis making it upper triangular. Appending $w$ gives an orthonormal basis of $V$ in which $[T]$ is upper triangular, since the first column has zeros below the diagonal by construction. ∎
Why start with $T^*$, not $T$. Taking an eigenvector of $T^*$ makes $W^\perp$ invariant under $T$, which is the direction the induction needs. Starting from an eigenvector of $T$ makes $W$ invariant instead, and $W^\perp$ need not be.

The complex spectral theorem

Theorem Spectral theorem, complex case

Let $T$ be an operator on a finite-dimensional complex inner product space. Then $T$ is normal if and only if $V$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $T$. In matrix terms: $A$ is normal iff $A=UDU^*$ with $U$ unitary and $D$ diagonal.

Proof Schur plus normality
(⇐) If $[T]=D$ is diagonal in an orthonormal basis then $[T^*]=\overline{D}$, and diagonal matrices commute, so $T$ is normal.
(⇒) By Schur, take an orthonormal basis with $A=[T]$ upper triangular. Compare the $(1,1)$ entries of $A^*A$ and $AA^*$: the first is $|A_{11}|^2$, the second is $\sum_j|A_{1j}|^2$.
Normality forces these equal, so $A_{1j}=0$ for all $j>1$: the first row is zero off the diagonal. Repeating with the $(2,2)$ entries kills the second row, and so on. The triangular matrix is diagonal. ∎
Example Diagonalize a unitary

$U=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&i\\i&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ is unitary, hence normal.

$f(t)=t^2+1$, so the eigenvalues are $\pm i$ — on the unit circle, as Unit 4 requires.
Eigenvectors: $(1,1)$ for $i$ and $(1,-1)$ for $-i$. They are orthogonal, as normality guarantees. Normalising and using them as columns of $U_0$ gives $U_0^*UU_0 =\left(\begin{smallmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

The real spectral theorem

Theorem Spectral theorem, real case

Let $T$ be an operator on a finite-dimensional real inner product space. Then $T$ is self-adjoint if and only if $V$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. In matrix terms: a real matrix is orthogonally diagonalizable iff it is symmetric, $A=QDQ^t$ with $Q$ orthogonal and $D$ real diagonal.

Proof Induction, using Unit 5
Unit 5 gave a real eigenvalue $\lambda$ with a real unit eigenvector $v$.
$\{v\}^\perp$ is $T$-invariant: for $w\perp v$, $\langle Tw,v\rangle=\langle w,Tv\rangle=\lambda\langle w,v\rangle=0$, using $T=T^*$.
The restriction of $T$ to $\{v\}^\perp$ is still self-adjoint, so induction gives an orthonormal eigenbasis there. Adjoining $v$ finishes. ∎
Normal is not enough over $\mathbb{R}$

The rotation $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ is normal — indeed orthogonal — but has no real eigenvalue, so no real eigenbasis exists. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the correct hypothesis is symmetry, not normality. The real classification of normal operators instead gives a block form with $2\times2$ rotation blocks.

Example Orthogonally diagonalize by hand

$A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, symmetric.

$f(t)=(2-t)^2-1=t^2-4t+3$, so eigenvalues $3$ and $1$.
$E_3=\operatorname{span}\{(1,1)\}$, $E_1=\operatorname{span}\{(1,-1)\}$ — orthogonal, as the theorem promises.
Normalise into $Q=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{smallmatrix} \right)$. Then $Q^tAQ=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{smallmatrix} \right)$, and $Q^t$ is the inverse for free.

Spectral resolution

Definition

Let $T$ be normal (complex) or self-adjoint (real) with distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_k$ and eigenspaces $E_1,\dots,E_k$. Writing $P_i$ for the orthogonal projection onto $E_i$, the spectral resolution is

$T=\sum_{i=1}^k\lambda_iP_i$, with $\sum_iP_i=I$, $P_iP_j=0$ for $i\ne j$, and $P_i^*=P_i=P_i^2$.

Equivalently $V=E_1\oplus\cdots\oplus E_k$, an orthogonal direct sum.

Theorem Functions of an operator

For any polynomial $p$, $p(T)=\sum_i p(\lambda_i)P_i$. More generally this defines $f(T)$ for any function $f$ on the spectrum — including $\sqrt{T}$ when the eigenvalues are non-negative, which Unit 7 needs.

Since $P_iP_j=0$ for $i\ne j$ and $P_i^2=P_i$, powers telescope: $T^m=\sum_i\lambda_i^mP_i$. Extend by linearity.

Example Resolve the last example

For $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ with eigenvalues $3,1$:

$P_3=\tfrac12\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and $P_1=\tfrac12\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Check $P_3+P_1=I$ and $P_3P_1=0$. ✓
$3P_3+1\cdot P_1 =\tfrac12\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3+1&3-1\\3-1&3+1\end{smallmatrix}\right) =\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)=A$. ✓
And $\sqrt{A}=\sqrt3P_3+P_1$, whose square is $A$ — a square root obtained without solving any equations.
Common pitfalls

• Quoting "normal implies diagonalizable" over $\mathbb{R}$. Only symmetry works there.
• Forgetting to normalise the eigenvectors. $Q$ must be orthogonal, so its columns need norm $1$; otherwise $Q^t\ne Q^{-1}$.
• Assuming eigenvectors within a single eigenspace are automatically orthogonal. Vectors from distinct eigenvalues are; inside a repeated eigenspace you must run Gram–Schmidt yourself.
• Writing $A=QDQ^{-1}$ and stopping. The content of the theorem is that $Q$ can be taken orthogonal or unitary, so $Q^{-1}=Q^*$.

Practice

Original problems written for this guide in the style of Friedberg, Insel & Spence exercises. Write a full solution before opening the answer.

Practice 1 · Orthogonal diagonalization

Orthogonally diagonalize $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

Answer
Eigenvalues $4$ and $2$ with eigenvectors $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$. With $Q=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{smallmatrix} \right)$, $Q^tAQ=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&0\\0&2\end{smallmatrix} \right)$.
Practice 2 · Repeated eigenvalue

Orthogonally diagonalize the $3\times3$ matrix with all entries $1$.

Solution
Eigenvalue $3$ with eigenvector $(1,1,1)$, and eigenvalue $0$ with the plane $x+y+z=0$. Run Gram–Schmidt inside that plane on $(1,-1,0)$ and $(1,0,-1)$ to get an orthonormal pair, then normalise $(1,1,1)$. The repeated eigenvalue is where the extra work lives.
Practice 3 · Which theorem applies

Can $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-2\\2&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ be orthogonally diagonalized over $\mathbb{R}$?

Answer
No. It is normal but not symmetric, and its characteristic polynomial $t^2+4$ has no real root. Over $\mathbb{C}$ it is unitarily diagonalizable with eigenvalues $\pm2i$.
Practice 4 · Schur by hand

Find a unitary $U$ making $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ upper triangular.

Solution
It already is upper triangular, so $U=I$ works. Note $A$ is not normal, so no unitary makes it diagonal — Schur is strictly weaker than the spectral theorem.
Practice 5 · Spectral resolution

Write the spectral resolution of $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}5&0\\0&5\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

Solution
One eigenvalue $5$ with $E_5=\mathbb{R}^2$, so $P_1=I$ and $T=5I$. A single term — scalar operators are the degenerate case.
Practice 6 · Square root

Find a self-adjoint square root of $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

Solution
From the resolution $A=3P_3+P_1$, take $\sqrt A=\sqrt3P_3+P_1 =\tfrac12\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\sqrt3+1&\sqrt3-1\\ \sqrt3-1&\sqrt3+1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Squaring returns $A$.
Practice 7 · Converse direction

Prove that if $A=UDU^*$ with $U$ unitary and $D$ diagonal then $A$ is normal.

Solution
$A^*=UD^*U^*$, so $A^*A=UD^*DU^*$ and $AA^*=UDD^*U^*$. Diagonal matrices commute, so $D^*D=DD^*$ and the two agree.
Practice 8 · Commuting operators

If $S$ and $T$ are commuting self-adjoint operators, show they are simultaneously orthogonally diagonalizable.

Solution
Each eigenspace $E_i$ of $T$ is $S$-invariant: if $Tv=\lambda v$ then $T(Sv)=S(Tv)=\lambda Sv$. Restrict $S$ to each $E_i$, where it is still self-adjoint, and apply the spectral theorem inside. Collecting the bases gives one orthonormal basis diagonalizing both.

Core theorem map

Schur triangularizes any complex operator; normality then forces the triangle to be diagonal. Over $\mathbb{R}$, Unit 5's existence result plus invariance of $v^\perp$ runs the same induction for symmetric operators.

Study routine. After every orthogonal diagonalization, verify $Q^tQ=I$ and recompute $QDQ^t$. Both checks are quick and catch the normalisation slip that costs the most marks.

Deep practice extensions

Use these after the main practice set. They are designed to force definition-level reasoning, theorem selection, and mixed application rather than routine calculation only.

Extension 1 · Hypothesis by field

What hypothesis does the spectral theorem need over $\mathbb{C}$, and over $\mathbb{R}$?

Guide
Normal over $\mathbb{C}$; self-adjoint over $\mathbb{R}$. Normality alone is not enough over $\mathbb{R}$, as rotations show.
Extension 2 · Role of Schur

Why prove Schur's theorem first?

Guide
It supplies an orthonormal basis with $[T]$ upper triangular for any complex operator. Normality is then only needed to knock out the entries above the diagonal.
Extension 3 · Matrix statement

Write the theorem as a factorization.

Guide
$A=UDU^*$ with $U$ unitary, $D$ diagonal, over $\mathbb{C}$ for normal $A$; and $A=QDQ^t$ with $Q$ orthogonal, $D$ real diagonal, over $\mathbb{R}$ for symmetric $A$.
Extension 4 · Using the resolution

Given $T=\sum\lambda_iP_i$, what is $T^{10}$?

Guide
$\sum\lambda_i^{10}P_i$. The projections are orthogonal idempotents, so cross terms vanish and only the eigenvalues get raised to the power.
