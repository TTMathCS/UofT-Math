Writing the Essay

The practical unit. How to get from a set topic to a submitted essay: what to do first, how to quote and cite without error, and what revision actually consists of once the argument exists.

By the end you can
  • Read an essay topic for what it actually asks.
  • Quote and cite accurately in the required style.
  • Revise for argument before revising for sentences.
  • Avoid the academic-integrity failures that are easiest to commit by accident.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Most marks lost in first-year English are not lost to weak ideas. They go to unanswered questions, unsupported claims and essays that stopped at the first draft.

In the real world

The discipline of drafting, leaving alone and returning transfers to every kind of writing. So does the habit of separating structural revision from sentence-level editing.

Story & open questions

Citation conventions exist so a reader can verify a claim. That is the whole purpose, and it explains most of the rules — including why a plausible-looking but non-existent source is a serious problem rather than a formatting slip.

Starting

Rule Read the topic properly
Underline the verb. "Compare" is not "discuss"; "account for" is not "describe". Marks are lost to the wrong verb more often than to anything else.
Note every constraint: which texts, how many, what length.
Identify what the topic is not asking. Half a good idea belongs in a different essay.
Example What a topic permits

"Discuss the use of silence in one of the texts studied."

One text. Writing on two is not generosity, it is ignoring the instruction and halving the depth available.
"Silence" needs defining — literal silences, unspoken subjects, gaps in narration. Choosing which, and saying so, is the first move.
"Discuss" still requires an argument. No topic verb ever means "summarise".
Rule Work from evidence to thesis

Do not decide the thesis first and hunt for support. Gather the passages that struck you, look for the pattern, then write the claim the pattern supports. The essays that fail are usually the ones that knew their conclusion before rereading.

Quoting

Rule Mechanics
Integrate short quotations into your own sentence grammatically. The result must read as a sentence.
Use square brackets for any change you make, and an ellipsis for anything omitted.
Quote poetry with a slash for line breaks, or set out longer passages as they appear.
Reproduce spelling and punctuation exactly, including anything that looks like an error.
Example Integration
Dropped in: The narrator is evasive. "I do not recall the evening." This leaves the quotation stranded.
Integrated: The narrator's evasion is grammatical as much as factual: he "do[es] not recall the evening", a phrasing that denies memory without denying the event.
The second version makes the quotation part of the argument's sentence, and the bracket shows the one word changed.
Never quote from memory

Check every quotation against the text before submitting. A misquotation undermines an argument built on the exact words, and it is the easiest error to prevent.

Citing

Rule Cite whatever is not yours
Direct quotations, obviously.
Paraphrases. Putting someone's idea in your own words still requires attribution.
Ideas and structures of argument taken from criticism.
Not required: common knowledge, and your own reading.

English courses normally use MLA, but the course sets the style. Check Quercus rather than assuming.

The failures that happen by accident
Patchwriting. Rearranging a source's sentence and citing it. If the structure is theirs, quote it or rewrite it properly.
Uncited paraphrase. The most common integrity breach in first year, and usually not deliberate.
Fabricated citation. A source that does not exist, or a real source that does not say what is claimed. Verifiable by anyone in seconds.
Unacknowledged generated text. Institutional policy governs this; check what the course permits.

The safe rule is simple: if a thought came from somewhere else, say where.

Revising

Rule Two passes, in order
Structural. Does the thesis hold? Does each paragraph advance it? Is the order right? This may mean cutting a paragraph you like.
Sentence-level. Only after the structure is settled. Polishing a paragraph you will delete is wasted work.
Example A revision checklist that works
Read only the first sentence of each paragraph in sequence. Do they form an argument? If they read as a list, the structure is wrong.
Check every claim has evidence, and every quotation has analysis after it.
Search for "very", "really", "clearly", "obviously". Each usually marks a claim that was not argued.
Read the essay aloud. Sentences that cannot be said are sentences that should be rewritten.
Confirm the essay answers the topic's verb.
Rule Leave time between draft and revision

A draft finished the night before submission cannot be revised, only proofread. Finish two or three days early and the same effort produces a noticeably better essay — because you return as a reader rather than as the writer.

Example Sentences worth fixing
Hedging: "It could perhaps be argued that the poem might suggest..." Say it or do not.
Filler openings: "In today's society" and "Throughout history" say nothing. Begin with the text.
Signposting the obvious: "In this essay I will argue" — just argue it.
Vague nouns: "aspects", "elements", "things". Name what you mean.
Common pitfalls

• Answering a topic you were not set.
• Quoting from memory.
• Paraphrasing a critic without citation.
• Editing sentences before the structure is settled.
• Submitting a first draft, which is the most reliable way to lose marks on an essay whose ideas were sound.

Practice

Work each fully before opening the answer. These are process questions and apply to every essay you will write.

Practice 1 · The verb

Why underline the topic's verb?

Answer
It specifies the task. "Compare" requires sustained comparison; "account for" requires explanation. Answering with the wrong verb loses marks however good the analysis.
Practice 2 · Integration

Fix: The imagery is violent. "the sky broke open"

Answer
"The imagery turns weather into injury: the sky 'broke open', a verb that belongs to bodies rather than to clouds." The quotation now sits inside the argument's sentence.
Practice 3 · Citation scope

Do you cite a paraphrase?

Answer
Yes. The idea is still someone else's. Changing the words does not change the ownership.
Practice 4 · Patchwriting

What is wrong with rearranging a source's sentence and citing it?

Answer
The structure and phrasing are still theirs, so the citation misrepresents how much is borrowed. Either quote it properly or rewrite it from your own understanding.
Practice 5 · Revision order

Why revise structure before sentences?

Answer
Because structural revision may delete the paragraph you polished. Sentence work is only efficient once you know what is staying.
Practice 6 · The topic-sentence test

You read your first sentences in sequence and they form a list. What does that show?

Solution
The paragraphs are not building on each other. Either reorder so each depends on the last, or the thesis is not doing enough work to connect them.
Practice 7 · Hedging

Rewrite: "It could be argued that the ending is perhaps somewhat ambiguous."

Answer
"The ending is ambiguous" — then show it. Hedging usually signals that the evidence was never gathered.
Practice 8 · Timing

Why finish a draft several days early?

Answer
So you return to it as a reader rather than as the person who wrote it. Distance is what makes the structural problems visible, and no amount of care at the keyboard substitutes for it.

Expanded topic checklist

Course coverage. This unit closes the ENG140Y1 topic map. It should cover the following:

  • Topics. Reading the verb and the constraints.
  • Quotation. Integration, brackets, ellipses, accuracy.
  • Citation. What requires it, and the accidental failures.
  • Revision. Structure first, sentences second, with time between.
Textbook/source alignment

Citation style, essay length and the marking rubric are all set by the course. Check Quercus, and treat the syllabus as authoritative wherever it differs from this guide. The Writing Centres offer free appointments and are the most underused resource at the university.

Core idea map

Units 1 to 8 produce readings. Unit 9 turns them into an argument. This unit turns the argument into a submitted essay, which is a separate skill and the one most often left to the last night.

Study routine. Book a Writing Centre appointment for your first essay of the year, before you need one. Bring a thesis and an outline rather than a finished draft — that is when the advice is worth most.

Extension 1 · Topics

What is the first thing to do with an essay topic?

Guide
Identify the verb and every constraint. The verb defines the task.
Extension 2 · Citation

What must be cited besides quotations?

Guide
Paraphrases, borrowed ideas, and argumentative structures taken from criticism.
Extension 3 · Revision

Which pass comes first?

Guide
Structural. Sentence editing is wasted on paragraphs that will be cut.
Extension 4 · Hedging

What does hedged phrasing usually indicate?

Guide
That the claim was not argued. Fix the evidence rather than softening the sentence.
