Improper Integrals

Integrals over infinite intervals, or of functions that blow up, defined carefully as limits. The recurring question is always the same: does the limit exist? The tests here are a direct rehearsal for the series tests two units from now.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Many vital integrals run to infinity or have a singularity — probability distributions, decaying signals, gravitational potentials. Improper integrals give them a rigorous meaning by taking a limit of ordinary, proper integrals.

In the real world

The Gaussian integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx=\sqrt{\pi}$ normalizes the bell curve; Laplace and Fourier transforms over infinite domains power signal processing and control engineering; the exponential waiting-time distribution in queueing theory is defined by one.

Story & open questions

Torricelli's "Gabriel's Horn" (1640s) has finite volume but infinite surface area — fillable with a finite amount of paint, yet impossible to paint. A vivid early warning that infinity defies intuition, and a direct ancestor of the $p$-test below.

By the end you can
  • Evaluate type I (infinite interval) and type II (singular) improper integrals as limits.
  • Apply the $p$-test to both.
  • Use direct and limit comparison to decide convergence without evaluating.
  • Explain the difference between convergence and absolute convergence for improper integrals.

Definitions and the $p$-test

Definition

$\displaystyle\int_a^\infty f=\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_a^t f$. If $f$ is unbounded near $b$, $\displaystyle\int_a^b f=\lim_{t\to b^-}\int_a^t f$. The integral converges exactly when this limit exists (as a finite number); otherwise it diverges. An integral improper at both ends is split at any interior point, and both pieces must converge.

$p$-test

$\displaystyle\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,dx$ converges iff $p>1$; $\displaystyle\int_0^1 x^{-p}\,dx$ converges iff $p<1$. The two cases are mirror images: near infinity, large $p$ decays fast enough; near $0$, only small $p$ keeps the blow-up mild enough.

Proof of the type I case
For $p\ne1$: $\displaystyle\int_1^t x^{-p}dx= \dfrac{t^{1-p}-1}{1-p}$. As $t\to\infty$, $t^{1-p}\to0$ iff $1-p<0$, i.e. $p>1$.
For $p=1$: $\int_1^t x^{-1}dx=\ln t\to\infty$, so it diverges — the boundary case fails.

Comparison tests

Direct comparison

If $0\le f\le g$ on $[a,\infty)$ and $\int_a^\infty g$ converges, so does $\int_a^\infty f$; if $\int_a^\infty f$ diverges, so does $\int_a^\infty g$. (Squeeze a nonnegative function between $0$ and something integrable, or below something that blows up.)

Limit comparison

If $f,g\ge0$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}=L$ with $0<L<\infty$, then $\int_a^\infty f$ and $\int_a^\infty g$ either both converge or both diverge. Useful when direct comparison is awkward to set up.

Example Direct comparison

$\int_1^\infty\dfrac{dx}{x^2+1}$ converges: $0\le\dfrac1{x^2+1}\le \dfrac1{x^2}$ and $\int_1^\infty x^{-2}dx$ converges ($p=2>1$).

Example Limit comparison

$\int_1^\infty\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^5+1}}\,dx$: compare with $g(x)=x^{-3/2}$.

$\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\dfrac{x\cdot x^{3/2}}{\sqrt{x^5+1}}= \dfrac{x^{5/2}}{\sqrt{x^5+1}}\to1$ as $x\to\infty$.
Since $\int_1^\infty x^{-3/2}dx$ converges ($p=3/2>1$), so does the original.
Absolute convergence If $\int_a^\infty|f|$ converges, then $\int_a^\infty f$ converges too — this lets comparison tests (which need $f\ge0$) apply indirectly to signed functions, exactly as with series later. The converse can fail: an integral can converge only conditionally.
Common pitfalls

• Always rewrite as a limit; never substitute $\infty$ or the singular point directly into the antiderivative.
• Watch for a hidden singularity inside the interval, not just at the endpoints — split the integral there.
• Comparison (direct or limit) needs eventually nonnegative functions.
• Limit comparison requires the limit to be a positive finite number — $L=0$ or $L=\infty$ only give one-directional information.

Practice

Decide convergence before you try to evaluate — often you don't need the antiderivative at all.

Practice 1

$\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\,dx$.

Answer
$[-e^{-x}]_0^\infty=1$.
Practice 2

Does $\int_1^\infty\dfrac{dx}{x}$ converge?

Answer
No; $\ln t\to\infty$ ($p=1$, the boundary case).
Practice 3

Converge or diverge: $\int_1^\infty\dfrac{2+\sin x}{x^2}\,dx$?

Answer
$0\le \tfrac{2+\sin x}{x^2}\le\tfrac{3}{x^2}$, and $\int x^{-2}dx$ converges, so it converges by direct comparison.
Practice 4 · Type II

Does $\int_0^1\dfrac{dx}{\sqrt x}$ converge? Evaluate it.

Solution
$p=1/2<1$, converges. $\lim_{t\to0^+}\int_t^1 x^{-1/2}dx=\lim_{t\to0^+}(2-2\sqrt t)=2$.
Practice 5 · Both ends

Does $\int_0^\infty\dfrac{dx}{\sqrt x(1+x)}$ converge?

Solution
Split at $1$. Near $0$, behaves like $x^{-1/2}$ ($p=1/2<1$, converges). Near $\infty$, behaves like $x^{-3/2}$ ($p=3/2>1$, converges). Both pieces converge, so the whole integral converges.
Practice 6 · Limit comparison

Does $\int_2^\infty\dfrac{\ln x}{x^2}\,dx$ converge?

Hint
For any $\varepsilon>0$, eventually $\ln x\le x^\varepsilon$, so compare with $x^{-2+\varepsilon}$ for small $\varepsilon$; it converges (and can also be evaluated directly by parts).
Practice 7 · Absolute convergence

Show $\int_1^\infty\dfrac{\cos x}{x^2}\,dx$ converges.

Answer
$\left|\dfrac{\cos x} {x^2}\right|\le\dfrac{1}{x^2}$, so the integral converges absolutely, hence converges.
Practice 8 · Gabriel's Horn

The horn formed by rotating $y=1/x$ for $x\ge1$ has volume $\int_1^\infty\pi/x^2\,dx$ and surface area at least $\int_1^\infty2\pi/x\,dx$. Show the volume is finite but the surface area is infinite.

Answer
Volume: $p=2>1$, converges to $\pi$. Surface area: bounded below by $\int 2\pi/x\,dx$, which is the $p=1$ boundary case and diverges — so the true surface area is infinite.
