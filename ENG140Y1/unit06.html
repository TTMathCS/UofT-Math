Unit 6 · Literature for our Time

Drama

A play is a script for something that has not happened yet. Reading one means holding two things at once: the words on the page, and the performance they are instructions for. Almost everything distinctive about dramatic analysis follows from that.

By the end you can
  • Read stage directions as meaningful, not incidental.
  • Analyse dialogue as action rather than information.
  • Use dramatic irony and subtext precisely.
  • Say what a staging choice would commit an interpretation to.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Drama has no narrator. Nobody explains what a character feels, so everything must be inferred from what they say and do in front of others — which is also how people are read in life.

In the real world

The techniques transfer to film and television, and to any situation where what is said and what is meant come apart. Subtext is a workplace skill as much as a literary one.

Story & open questions

Whether a play is its text or only its performances is a genuine dispute. A novel is fixed; a play exists differently in every production, and a reading that ignores staging misses what the form is for.

What is on the page

Definition
Dialogue: what characters say.
Stage directions: instructions for movement, setting, sound, lighting and delivery.
Aside: speech the audience hears and other characters do not.
Soliloquy: a character alone, speaking their thoughts.
Monologue: a long uninterrupted speech to others.
Rule Stage directions are text

They are not scaffolding to be skipped. A playwright who specifies that a character does not look up has made a choice as deliberate as any line.

Sparse directions leave staging open and hand authority to the director.
Extremely detailed ones assert control, and sometimes read as prose in their own right.
A direction that cannot be staged — an instruction about what a character is thinking — is worth noticing precisely because it exceeds the form.
Example A direction doing the work

An invented exchange:

She: "I'm glad you came." (She does not move from the door.)
The line alone is welcome. The direction contradicts it, and the contradiction is the scene.
Nothing in the dialogue tells you the welcome is unmeant. An analysis that quoted only the speech would get the moment exactly backwards.

Dialogue as action

Rule Speech does things

In drama, a line is a move. Ask what each speech attempts: to persuade, deflect, wound, delay, claim ground. Characters rarely speak to inform.

Definition

Subtext is what is meant beneath what is said. It exists wherever the surface of the dialogue does not account for the scene.

Example Reading subtext

A: "You've rearranged the kitchen." B: "I've had time."

On the surface: an observation and an explanation.
Underneath: A notices change and does not approve. B hears the criticism and answers it with a reproach about absence.
Neither says any of that. The evidence is the mismatch — "I've had time" is not a response to the literal remark, so it must be answering something else.
That is how subtext is argued: point at the gap between what was said and what was answered.
Rule Also read the shape of the talk
Who speaks most, and who interrupts?
Who asks questions and who answers, and who evades them?
Where are the silences, and who is left holding them?

Power in a scene is usually visible in the distribution of speech before it is visible in the content.

Irony and structure

Definition

Dramatic irony occurs when the audience knows something a character does not. Every line the character speaks then carries a second meaning they cannot hear.

Example Why it is a dramatic device specifically
A novel can produce it, but drama does so constantly, because an audience watches several characters at once and accumulates what no single one knows.
Once established, ordinary lines become unbearable. A character saying "we have plenty of time" means one thing to them and another to everyone watching.
To write about it, identify the moment the audience gains the knowledge, then trace what it does to the lines that follow.
Rule Structure in performance
Acts and scenes are units of attention. A scene break is a change of place, time or pressure.
Entrances and exits alter the configuration of who can hear what. A character arriving changes what may be said.
Real time. Unlike prose, a play mostly runs at the speed of speech, so a playwright compresses by choosing which moments to stage at all.

Reading with performance in mind

Rule Ambiguity is a resource

A line can often be delivered several ways, and the text does not always decide. That is not a gap to be filled but a fact about the form.

The useful move is to name the possibilities and say what each would commit the production to.
"If delivered flatly the line reads as resignation; if delivered sharply it reads as accusation, and the scene's ending changes accordingly." That is an argument, and it stays within the evidence.
Example Staging as interpretation

A play ends with a character alone on stage and no closing line.

Bring the lights down slowly and it reads as diminishment.
Cut to black instantly and it reads as interruption — something stopped rather than ended.
Leave the lights up past comfort and it becomes the audience's problem, which implicates them.

The script permits all three. Naming that, and arguing which the text best supports, is stronger than pretending it settles the question.

Do not treat characters as psychological case studies

The same rule as Unit 5, and it bites harder here because drama gives you no interiority at all. Write about what a character says and does and what the play makes of it — not what they "really" feel, which the form deliberately withholds.

Common pitfalls

• Skipping stage directions.
• Treating dialogue as information rather than action.
• Claiming subtext without pointing at the mismatch that reveals it.
• Ignoring that the text is a script for performance.
• Analysing a play exactly as if it were a novel with the narration removed.

Practice

Work each fully before opening the answer. These use invented exchanges so the method is visible without depending on a set text.

Practice 1 · Terms

What separates a soliloquy from a monologue?

Answer
A soliloquy is spoken alone, so the audience hears private thought. A monologue is a long speech delivered to others, so it is performed and may be untrue.
Practice 2 · Direction

What does a direction like (pause) before a reply do?

Answer
Marks the reply as considered rather than immediate, which usually means something is being weighed or withheld. It also gives the audience time to notice the hesitation.
Practice 3 · Subtext

Find it: A: "Are you staying?" B: "The trains run till midnight."

Answer
B has not answered. Supplying the departure information instead announces an intention to leave while avoiding the word, so the refusal is delivered without ever being stated — and A must decide whether to press it.
Practice 4 · Speech distribution

One character has forty lines in a scene, the other four. What might that show?

Answer
Usually a power imbalance — though which way is arguable. Volume can mean control, or it can mean anxiety filling a silence the other is comfortable holding. Argue from what the four lines do.
Practice 5 · Dramatic irony

How would you write about a scene once the audience knows more than the character?

Solution
Locate the moment the audience gained the knowledge, then show how specific subsequent lines acquire a second meaning. Quote a line whose surface sense and audience sense diverge — that divergence is the evidence.
Practice 6 · Entrance

Why does a character's entrance mid-scene matter?

Answer
It changes who can hear what, so it changes what may be said. Conversations often break off or shift register, and the shift shows what was private.
Practice 7 · Ambiguous line

A line could be sincere or sarcastic and the text does not say. How do you handle it?

Answer
Name both readings and say what each commits the scene to, then argue which the surrounding text better supports. Do not pretend the ambiguity is absent.
Practice 8 · Silence

Can a silence be quoted as evidence?

Answer
Yes, when the script marks it. A written pause is as much a textual choice as a word, and often the more pointed one.

Core idea map

No narrator, so everything is inference from speech and action. Stage directions are text. Subtext is argued from mismatch. The script underdetermines the performance, and saying so precisely is part of the analysis.

Study routine. Read at least one scene aloud, taking all the parts. Pace, interruption and the weight of a pause are audible and nearly invisible on a silent page.

Deep practice extensions

Use these after the main practice set. They test whether the distinctions have actually landed.

Extension 1 · Directions

Are stage directions part of the text?

Guide
Yes, and often the most pointed part. They can contradict the dialogue, which is where scenes turn.
Extension 2 · Dialogue

What question should you ask of every speech?

Guide
What it is trying to do — persuade, deflect, wound, delay. Not what it reports.
Extension 3 · Evidencing subtext

How is subtext demonstrated?

Guide
By a mismatch: a reply that does not answer the question asked, or an action that contradicts the words.
Extension 4 · Ambiguity

What do you do with a line the text does not settle?

Guide
Name the possible deliveries, say what each would commit the production to, and argue which the text best supports.
