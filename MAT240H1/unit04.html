Linear Transformations

The maps that respect vector-space structure. They are the real subject of linear algebra — matrices are just the way we write them down once a basis is chosen.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Linearity — $T(av+bw)=aT(v)+bT(w)$ — is the structure-preserving condition. Studying the maps, not just the spaces, is what gives linear algebra its power.

In the real world

Every layer of a neural network is a linear transformation followed by a nonlinearity; rotations and projections in graphics are linear maps; so are the transforms of signal processing.

Story & open questions

The shift from "matrix as array of numbers" to "matrix as a linear map in coordinates" is the conceptual leap that organizes the whole subject and generalizes to infinite dimensions in functional analysis.

By the end you can
  • Define a linear transformation and verify linearity.
  • Describe the null space and range.
  • See how a basis turns a map into a matrix.

Definition and examples

Definition

$T:V\to W$ is linear if $T(av+bw)=aT(v)+bT(w)$ for all scalars $a,b$ and vectors $v,w$. Then $T(0)=0$ automatically.

Examples

Differentiation $D:P\to P$, multiplication by a matrix, rotation of the plane about the origin — all linear. Squaring is not.

Null space and range

Definition

The null space (kernel) is $\{v:T(v)=0\}$; the range is $\{T(v):v\in V\}$. Both are subspaces.

Common pitfalls

• Check both additivity and scalar-homogeneity for linearity.
• $T(0)=0$ is necessary, not sufficient.
• The matrix of $T$ depends on the chosen bases.

Practice

Practice 1

Is $T(x,y)=(x+y,\,2x)$ linear?

Answer
Yes — it is matrix multiplication by $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\2&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
Practice 2

Is $T(x)=x+1$ linear?

Answer
No — $T(0)=1\ne0$.
Practice 3

Find the null space of $T(x,y)=(x+y,\,x+y)$.

Answer
$\{(x,-x)\}$, a line.
