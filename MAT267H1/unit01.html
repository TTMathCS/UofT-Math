First Order Equations

The handful of first order equations that can be solved by an integral. This is the one unit that looks like the applied course, and it is worth doing carefully for a reason that only becomes clear in Unit 2: every method here is really a change of variables that turns the equation into $\frac{d}{dt}(\text{something})=0$, and knowing which "something" is the whole skill.

By the end you can
  • Recognise separable, linear, exact and homogeneous equations on sight.
  • Derive the integrating factor rather than quoting it.
  • Test exactness and construct the potential function.
  • Say what an implicit solution does and does not tell you.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Almost no differential equation has a closed-form solution. The ones in this unit are the exceptions, and they are the examples every later theorem is tested against — when Unit 2 claims a unique solution exists, you want equations where you can look at it.

In the real world

Linear first order equations are the whole of single-compartment modelling: drug clearance, RC circuits, radioactive decay, Newton's law of cooling. The logistic equation is separable, which is why population models start there.

Story & open questions

Integrating factors are Euler's, from the 1730s. The modern question is not how to find them by hand but when they exist at all: differential Galois theory settles which equations have solutions in elementary terms, and the answer is almost none.

What a first order equation asks

Definition Initial value problem

Given $f$ on a region of $\mathbb{R}^2$, a solution of

$y'=f(t,y),\qquad y(t_0)=y_0$

is a differentiable function $y$ on an interval $I$ containing $t_0$ with $(t,y(t))$ in the region and $y'(t)=f(t,y(t))$ for every $t\in I$. The interval is part of the answer. A "solution" with no interval attached is an incomplete statement.

Definition Direction field

At each point $(t,y)$ draw a short segment of slope $f(t,y)$. A solution is a curve whose tangent matches the field everywhere. Nothing about this picture needs a formula, which is exactly why it survives into the qualitative half of the course.

Autonomous or not. If $f(t,y)=f(y)$ the equation is autonomous and the direction field does not change with $t$: shifting a solution in time gives another solution. That single fact is what makes phase-line and phase-plane pictures possible in Units 8-10.

Separable equations

Method Separation of variables

If $y'=g(t)h(y)$ and $h(y_0)\ne0$, then near $t_0$

$\int\frac{dy}{h(y)}=\int g(t)\,dt+C.$

The justification is the chain rule, not algebra with differentials: let $H$ be an antiderivative of $1/h$. Then $\frac{d}{dt}H(y(t))=y'/h(y)=g(t)$, so $H(y(t))-\int g$ is constant. The condition $h(y_0)\ne0$ is what keeps $1/h$ integrable near the starting value.

Example Logistic growth

$y'=ry(1-y/K)$ with $0<y_0<K$. Partial fractions give

$\int\Big(\frac1y+\frac{1/K}{1-y/K}\Big)dy=rt+C \implies\ln\frac{y}{1-y/K}=rt+C,$

so $y(t)=\dfrac{K}{1+Ae^{-rt}}$ with $A=(K-y_0)/y_0$. The two constant solutions $y\equiv0$ and $y\equiv K$ are exactly the roots of $h$, the values separation had to exclude.

Where the constant solutions go

Dividing by $h(y)$ deletes every root of $h$ from the solution set. Those roots are equilibria and are usually the most important solutions in the problem. Always list them separately before dividing.

Linear equations and the integrating factor

Theorem First order linear

For $y'+p(t)y=q(t)$ with $p,q$ continuous on an interval $I$ containing $t_0$, set $\mu(t)=\exp\big(\int_{t_0}^tp(s)\,ds\big)$. Then $(\mu y)'=\mu(y'+py)=\mu q$, so

$y(t)=\frac{1}{\mu(t)}\Big(y_0+\int_{t_0}^t\mu(s)q(s)\,ds\Big),$

and this solution exists on all of $I$ — the whole interval where the coefficients are continuous, with no shrinking. Linear equations never blow up in finite time; Unit 3 shows how special that is.

Derivation Where $\mu$ comes from
Look for $\mu$ making the left side a single derivative: want $(\mu y)'=\mu y'+\mu'y$ to equal $\mu(y'+py)$.
Comparing the $y$ terms forces $\mu'=p\mu$, a separable equation for $\mu$ with solution $\mu=e^{\int p}$.
Any nonzero multiple of $\mu$ works, so no constant of integration is needed. ∎

Nothing was memorised. The factor is forced by asking the product rule to fit.

Example A drug dose

$y'=-ky+d$ with constant infusion $d$: $\mu=e^{kt}$, and

$y(t)=\frac dk+\Big(y_0-\frac dk\Big)e^{-kt}.$

Every solution runs to $d/k$ at rate $k$, whatever the starting dose. Unit 9 will call $d/k$ an asymptotically stable equilibrium; here you can just read it off.

Exact equations

Definition Exact

$M(t,y)\,dt+N(t,y)\,dy=0$ is exact on a region if there is $F$ with $F_t=M$ and $F_y=N$. Then $\frac{d}{dt}F(t,y(t))=M+Ny'=0$, so solutions are the level curves $F(t,y)=C$.

Theorem Exactness test

On a simply connected region with $M,N$ continuously differentiable, the equation is exact if and only if

$\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial N}{\partial t}.$

Necessity is equality of mixed partials for $F$. Sufficiency is the Poincaré lemma; MAT257Y1 proves it in the language of closed and exact forms, and this is the same statement in dimension two.

Example Building $F$

$(2ty+1)\,dt+(t^2+3y^2)\,dy=0$ has $M_y=2t=N_t$, so it is exact. Integrate $M$ in $t$: $F=t^2y+t+\varphi(y)$. Differentiate in $y$: $t^2+\varphi'(y)=N=t^2+3y^2$, so $\varphi=y^3$ and

$F(t,y)=t^2y+t+y^3=C.$

The solution is implicit and that is all you get. Solving for $y$ would mean solving a cubic, and past this example it is usually impossible.

Example Integrating factor, general form

$y\,dt+2t\,dy=0$ is not exact: $M_y=1$, $N_t=2$. Multiplying by $\mu(t,y)$ and asking for exactness gives a partial differential equation for $\mu$ in general, but if the combination $(M_y-N_t)/N$ depends on $t$ alone the factor is a function of $t$ alone. Here $(1-2)/(2t)=-1/(2t)$, giving $\mu=t^{-1/2}$ and the exact equation $yt^{-1/2}dt+2t^{1/2}dy=0$ with $F=2t^{1/2}y$.

Everything here is one idea. Separable, linear and exact are all "find $F$ with $\frac{d}{dt}F=0$ along solutions". Unit 9 keeps the idea and drops the equality: a function that only decreases along solutions is a Lyapunov function, and it settles stability for equations nothing here can solve.
Common pitfalls

• Losing the equilibrium solutions when dividing by $h(y)$.
• Treating an implicit solution $F(t,y)=C$ as if it defined $y$ globally. It defines $y$ only near points where $F_y\ne0$ — the implicit function theorem again.
• Writing $\mu=e^{\int p}$ with $p$ discontinuous somewhere in the interval. The formula is valid only on an interval where $p$ is continuous.
• Applying the exactness test on a region with a hole. Simple connectedness is a real hypothesis.
• Reporting a solution with no interval of existence attached.

Practice

Original problems written for this guide in the style of Teschl's exercises. Write a full solution before opening the answer.

Practice 1 · Separable with equilibria

Solve $y'=y(1-y)$, $y(0)=\tfrac12$, and list every constant solution.

Solution
Constants are $y\equiv0$ and $y\equiv1$. Otherwise partial fractions give $\ln\frac{y}{1-y}=t+C$, and $y(0)=\tfrac12$ gives $C=0$, so $y=\dfrac{e^t}{1+e^t}$, rising from $0$ to $1$.
Practice 2 · Linear, non-constant coefficient

Solve $ty'+2y=t^3$ for $t>0$.

Solution
Divide by $t$: $y'+\frac2ty=t^2$, so $\mu=t^2$ and $(t^2y)'=t^4$. Then $y=\dfrac{t^3}{5}+\dfrac{C}{t^2}$. The interval must avoid $t=0$, where the coefficient blows up.
Practice 3 · Exactness

Is $(3t^2y+y^2)\,dt+(t^3+2ty)\,dy=0$ exact? Solve it.

Solution
$M_y=3t^2+2y=N_t$, so yes. Integrating $M$ in $t$ gives $F=t^3y+ty^2+\varphi(y)$; matching $F_y=t^3+2ty$ forces $\varphi'=0$. The solutions are $t^3y+ty^2=C$.
Practice 4 · Bernoulli

Solve $y'+y=y^3$ by substituting $v=y^{-2}$.

Solution
$v'=-2y^{-3}y'$, so dividing the equation by $y^3$ gives $-\tfrac12v'+v=1$, that is $v'-2v=-2$. Then $v=1+Ce^{2t}$ and $y=(1+Ce^{2t})^{-1/2}$. The substitution is the standard trick for $y'+py=qy^n$.
Practice 5 · Homogeneous of degree zero

Solve $y'=\dfrac{t+y}{t}$ for $t>0$.

Solution
The right side is $1+y/t$, a function of $y/t$ alone. Put $v=y/t$, so $y=tv$ and $y'=v+tv'$. Then $v+tv'=1+v$, giving $v'=1/t$, $v=\ln t+C$ and $y=t\ln t+Ct$.
Practice 6 · Interval of validity

Solve $y'=y^2$, $y(0)=y_0>0$, and give the largest interval on which the solution lives.

Solution
Separating, $y=\dfrac{y_0}{1-y_0t}$, valid on $(-\infty,1/y_0)$. The right side is a polynomial, perfectly smooth everywhere, and the solution still escapes in finite time. Smoothness of $f$ buys local existence only.
Practice 7 · Integrating factor in $y$

$2y\,dt+(t+y)\,dy=0$ is not exact. Find a factor depending on $y$ alone.

Solution
$M_y=2$, $N_t=1$, so $(N_t-M_y)/M=-1/(2y)$ depends on $y$ only, giving $\mu=y^{-1/2}$. The equation $2y^{1/2}dt+(t+y)y^{-1/2}dy=0$ is exact with $F=2ty^{1/2}+\tfrac23y^{3/2}$.
Practice 8 · Reading a direction field

For $y'=y-t$, which straight line is a solution, and what do nearby solutions do?

Solution
Try $y=t+1$: $y'=1$ and $y-t=1$, so it solves. The general solution is $y=t+1+Ce^t$, so every other solution leaves it exponentially fast. This is a repelling particular solution — the direction field shows it as the ridge trajectories fall away from.

Core theorem map

Every method here produces a conserved quantity. Unit 2 replaces "solve it" with "prove a solution exists", Unit 3 asks how long it lasts, and Unit 9 keeps only the conserved-quantity idea, weakened to a function that decreases.

Study routine. Classify before you compute. Given any first order equation, decide in one line whether it is separable, linear, exact, or none of the three — "none" is the common answer, and it is what makes the rest of the course necessary.

Deep practice extensions

Use these after the main practice set. They force definition-level reasoning and method selection rather than routine calculation.

Extension 1 · Why $\mu'=p\mu$

Explain in one sentence why the integrating factor solves that equation.

Guide
Because matching $(\mu y)'$ with $\mu(y'+py)$ forces the coefficients of $y$ to agree, and that comparison is $\mu'=p\mu$.
Extension 2 · Linear versus nonlinear

Why can a linear equation never blow up in finite time inside its coefficient interval?

Guide
The explicit formula involves only integrals of continuous functions on a compact subinterval, so it is bounded there. Nonlinear equations have no such formula and $y'=y^2$ shows what happens instead.
Extension 3 · Exactness and topology

Where does simple connectedness get used?

Guide
In building $F$ by integrating along a path: without it the integral can depend on the path. The angle form on the punctured plane is the standard counterexample.
Extension 4 · Implicit solutions

What does $F(t,y)=C$ tell you about $y$ near a point where $F_y=0$?

Guide
Nothing directly. The implicit function theorem needs $F_y\ne0$ to produce a function $y$ of $t$; where it vanishes the level curve can turn vertical or cross.
