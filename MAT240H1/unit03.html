Bases & Dimension

A basis is a minimal spanning set — just enough vectors to build everything, with none to spare. Its size, the dimension, is the same no matter which basis you choose.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

A basis gives every vector unique coordinates, turning an abstract space into something computable. Dimension is the single number that classifies the space.

In the real world

Choosing a basis is choosing coordinates — pixel bases for images, Fourier bases for audio, principal components for data. The right basis makes a problem easy.

Story & open questions

That every basis of a space has the same size is a theorem, not an assumption — the result that makes "dimension" well defined and underpins all of linear algebra.

By the end you can
  • Define a basis and find one for a given space.
  • Prove all bases have the same size.
  • Compute dimension and use it.

Basis and dimension

Definition

A basis is a linearly independent set that spans $V$. Every vector is then a unique linear combination of basis vectors. The dimension is the number of basis vectors.

Theorem Invariance of dimension

Any two bases of a finite-dimensional space have the same number of elements.

Using dimension

Facts

In an $n$-dimensional space: any $n$ independent vectors form a basis, any $n$ spanning vectors form a basis, and more than $n$ vectors must be dependent.

Example

$\dim\mathbb{R}^n=n$; $\dim P_k(\mathbb{R})=k+1$ (basis $1,x,\dots,x^k$).

Common pitfalls

• A basis must be both independent and spanning — neither alone suffices.
• Coordinates depend on the chosen basis and its order.
• Infinite-dimensional spaces (like all polynomials) have no finite basis.

Practice

Practice 1

Find a basis and the dimension of $\{(x,y,z):x+y+z=0\}$.

Answer
e.g. $(1,-1,0),(1,0,-1)$; dimension $2$.
Practice 2

What is $\dim$ of the space of $2\times2$ matrices?

Answer
$4$.
Practice 3

Can 4 vectors be independent in $\mathbb{R}^3$?

Answer
No — more than $\dim=3$ vectors are always dependent.
