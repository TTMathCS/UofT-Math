The Derivative as a Linear Map

In one variable the derivative is a number, and the number happens to be the slope of the best linear approximation. In $n$ variables the number is gone and only the approximation survives. The derivative of $f$ at $a$ is the linear map that fits $f$ best near $a$, and every theorem in the next five units is a statement about that map.

By the end you can
  • State differentiability as a limit that says "the error is $o(\|h\|)$".
  • Show the derivative is unique, and that it forces continuity.
  • Compute the Jacobian matrix and know when it represents a derivative.
  • Give the standard example where all partials exist and $f$ is still not differentiable.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Partial derivatives are easy to compute and prove almost nothing. They look along $n$ directions and say nothing about the other infinitely many. The linear-map definition is the one that supports a chain rule, an inverse function theorem, and integration on manifolds.

In the real world

Every gradient-based optimiser, every Newton step, and every backward pass in a neural network is this definition applied numerically. Automatic differentiation propagates linear maps, not numbers, which is exactly why it composes.

Story & open questions

Fréchet gave this definition in 1911, and it works verbatim in infinite-dimensional normed spaces, which is where it is now mostly used. The weaker Gâteaux derivative — directional derivatives in every direction — came from the same period and is genuinely weaker: the two disagree on functions you meet in this unit.

The definition

Definition Differentiable at a point

Let $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ be open, $f:A\to\mathbb{R}^m$, and $a\in A$. Then $f$ is differentiable at $a$ if there is a linear map $Df(a):\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ with

$\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0} \frac{\|f(a+h)-f(a)-Df(a)h\|}{\|h\|}=0.$

Equivalently $f(a+h)=f(a)+Df(a)h+r(h)$ where the remainder satisfies $r(h)/\|h\|\to0$. The whole content is that the error vanishes faster than linearly, not merely that it vanishes.

Theorem The derivative is unique

At most one linear map can satisfy the definition.

Suppose $L$ and $L'$ both work. Then $\|(L-L')h\|/\|h\|\to0$ as $h\to0$.
Fix a unit vector $u$ and put $h=tu$ with $t\to0$. By linearity $\|(L-L')(tu)\|/\|tu\|=\|(L-L')u\|$, a constant in $t$. A constant with limit $0$ is $0$.
So $(L-L')u=0$ for every unit $u$, hence $L=L'$. ∎
Theorem Differentiable implies continuous

$f(a+h)-f(a)=Df(a)h+r(h)$. As $h\to0$ the first term goes to $0$ because linear maps on $\mathbb{R}^n$ are bounded, and the second goes to $0$ because it is $o(\|h\|)$.

Read the definition as a claim about error. "Differentiable" means the affine function $x\mapsto f(a)+Df(a)(x-a)$ is not just close to $f$ near $a$ — it is close to first order. That single word is what all the later theorems consume.

Directional and partial derivatives

Definition

The directional derivative of $f$ at $a$ in direction $u$ is

$\displaystyle D_uf(a)=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{f(a+tu)-f(a)}{t},$

when the limit exists. Taking $u=e_j$, the $j$-th standard basis vector, gives the partial derivative $\partial f/\partial x_j$.

Theorem Differentiability determines all directional derivatives

If $f$ is differentiable at $a$ then $D_uf(a)=Df(a)u$ for every $u$.

Put $h=tu$ in the definition: $f(a+tu)-f(a)=tDf(a)u+r(tu)$, and $r(tu)/t\to0$ since $\|tu\|=|t|\,\|u\|$. Divide by $t$ and let $t\to0$.

So the derivative, a single linear map, packages every directional derivative at once. The converse is false, which is the point of the next box.

Example All directional derivatives, still not differentiable

Let $f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2y}{x^4+y^2}$ for $(x,y)\ne(0,0)$ and $f(0,0)=0$.

Along the line $(tu_1,tu_2)$ with $u_2\ne0$ the quotient is $t u_1^2u_2/(t^2u_1^4+u_2^2)\to0$, and along the $x$-axis it is $0$. So every directional derivative at the origin exists and equals $0$.
If $f$ were differentiable, $Df(0)$ would have to be the zero map, so $f(h)/\|h\|\to0$. But along the parabola $y=x^2$ we get $f(x,x^2)=\tfrac12$ for all $x\ne0$, so $f$ is not even continuous at the origin.

Directional derivatives probe along straight lines. Differentiability is a statement about every approach, including curved ones.

The Jacobian matrix

Definition

Write $f=(f_1,\dots,f_m)$. The Jacobian matrix of $f$ at $a$ is the $m\times n$ matrix $Jf(a)$ with entries $\partial f_i/\partial x_j$ evaluated at $a$.

If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $Jf(a)$ is the matrix of $Df(a)$ in the standard bases: column $j$ is $Df(a)e_j=\partial f/\partial x_j$.

Theorem Continuous partials imply differentiable

If every $\partial f_i/\partial x_j$ exists on a neighbourhood of $a$ and is continuous at $a$, then $f$ is differentiable at $a$. Such an $f$ is called $C^1$.

It is enough to prove it for $m=1$, since $f$ is differentiable exactly when each $f_i$ is.
Walk from $a$ to $a+h$ one coordinate at a time, giving $n$ increments. Each is a one-variable change, so the mean value theorem supplies a point $c_j$ on that segment with increment $\partial f/\partial x_j(c_j)\,h_j$.
Summing, $f(a+h)-f(a)=\sum_j\partial f/\partial x_j(c_j)\,h_j$. Subtract $\sum_j\partial f/\partial x_j(a)h_j$; each term is bounded by $|h_j|$ times the oscillation of a partial, which continuity makes small. Divide by $\|h\|\ge|h_j|$ and the quotient tends to $0$. ∎

This is the theorem you actually use. Nobody verifies the limit definition on a formula built from elementary functions — they check the partials are continuous and stop.

Example A Jacobian

For $f(x,y)=(x^2-y^2,\,2xy)$, the Jacobian is $\begin{smallmatrix}2x&-2y\\2y&2x\end{smallmatrix}$ with determinant $4(x^2+y^2)$. The partials are polynomials, so $f$ is $C^1$ everywhere and the matrix genuinely is $Df$. Note the determinant vanishes only at the origin — a fact Unit 6 will turn into a statement about invertibility.

The gradient, and what it is not

Definition

For $f:A\to\mathbb{R}$, the gradient $\nabla f(a)$ is the vector representing the linear functional $Df(a)$ through the dot product: $Df(a)h=\nabla f(a)\cdot h$.

Theorem Steepest ascent

Among unit vectors $u$, the directional derivative $D_uf(a)=\nabla f(a)\cdot u$ is largest when $u$ points along $\nabla f(a)$, by Cauchy–Schwarz, and the largest value is $\|\nabla f(a)\|$.

The gradient is a translation, not the derivative. $Df(a)$ is a map $\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$; $\nabla f(a)$ is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$. They correspond only because the dot product identifies a space with its dual. On a manifold with no preferred inner product that identification is not free, which is why Unit 11 works with the linear functional directly and calls it a $1$-form.
Common pitfalls

• Calling the matrix of partials "the derivative" without checking differentiability. The matrix can exist when the derivative does not.
• Testing differentiability along lines only. Curved approaches such as $y=x^2$ break examples that all lines pass.
• Forgetting the domain must be open. At a boundary point the limit $h\to0$ has no room to run in every direction.
• Writing $\nabla f$ for vector-valued $f$. The gradient is defined for scalar-valued functions; the general object is the Jacobian.
• Treating $C^1$ as equivalent to differentiable. It is strictly stronger: $x^2\sin(1/x)$ is differentiable everywhere with a discontinuous derivative.

Practice

Original problems written for this guide in the style of Spivak and Munkres exercises. Write a full solution before opening the answer.

Practice 1 · Straight from the definition

Show $f(x,y)=x^2+3xy$ is differentiable at $(1,2)$ and find $Df(1,2)$.

Solution
Partials are $2x+3y=8$ and $3x=3$, so the candidate is $L(h,k)=8h+3k$. Expanding, $f(1+h,2+k)-f(1,2)-8h-3k=h^2+3hk$, and $|h^2+3hk|\le4\|(h,k)\|^2$, which over $\|(h,k)\|$ tends to $0$. So $Df(1,2)=(8,3)$.
Practice 2 · Linear maps

What is the derivative of a linear map $L$ at any point?

Answer
$L$ itself. $L(a+h)-L(a)-Lh=0$ exactly, so the remainder is not merely $o(\|h\|)$ but identically zero. Affine maps behave the same way, with the constant dropping out.
Practice 3 · Partials exist, function is wild

Let $f(x,y)=1$ when $xy=0$ and $f(x,y)=0$ otherwise. What are the partials at the origin? Is $f$ differentiable there?

Solution
Both partials are $0$: along either axis $f$ is constantly $1$. But $f$ is discontinuous at the origin, so not differentiable. Partials see only the axes, and this function was designed to be constant exactly there.
Practice 4 · Norm at the origin

Is $f(x)=\|x\|$ differentiable at $0$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$? Elsewhere?

Solution
Not at $0$: the directional derivative in direction $u$ is $\|u\|=1$ and in direction $-u$ is also $1$, but a linear map must give opposite signs. Away from $0$ it is differentiable with $\nabla f(x)=x/\|x\|$, since $\|x\|=\sqrt{x\cdot x}$ is a composition of smooth maps where $x\ne0$.
Practice 5 · Products

Prove $f(x,y)=xy$ from $\mathbb{R}^2$ to $\mathbb{R}$ is differentiable with $Df(a,b)(h,k)=bh+ak$.

Solution
$(a+h)(b+k)-ab-bh-ak=hk$, and $|hk|\le\tfrac12\|(h,k)\|^2$, so the remainder over $\|(h,k)\|$ tends to $0$. This one computation is the product rule.
Practice 6 · Continuity is not enough

Give a continuous function on $\mathbb{R}^2$ that is not differentiable at the origin, with all partials existing there.

Solution
$f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^3}{x^2+y^2}$, with $f(0,0)=0$. It is continuous since $|f|\le\|(x,y)\|$. Partials at the origin are $1$ and $0$, so the candidate derivative is $(h,k)\mapsto h$; but $f(h,h)-h=-h/2$, which over $\|(h,h)\|$ does not tend to $0$.
Practice 7 · $C^1$ is strictly stronger

Show $f(x)=x^2\sin(1/x)$, $f(0)=0$, is differentiable everywhere but not $C^1$.

Solution
At $0$ the difference quotient is $x\sin(1/x)\to0$, so $f'(0)=0$. For $x\ne0$, $f'(x)=2x\sin(1/x)-\cos(1/x)$, which oscillates between roughly $-1$ and $1$ as $x\to0$ and so has no limit. The derivative exists everywhere and is discontinuous at one point.
Practice 8 · Jacobian of polar coordinates

For $g(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,\,r\sin\theta)$, compute $Jg$ and its determinant.

Solution
$Jg=\begin{smallmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{smallmatrix}$ with determinant $r$. It vanishes exactly at $r=0$, the one place polar coordinates fail to be a local change of variables — and the factor $r$ that appears in every polar integral in Unit 10.

Core theorem map

Differentiable gives continuous and gives all directional derivatives. Continuous partials give differentiable. Neither arrow reverses, and the counterexamples in this unit are the standard proofs of that.

Study routine. For each example you meet, decide which of the four boxes it lives in: partials exist or not, differentiable or not. The interesting functions are the ones in the "partials but no derivative" corner, and you should be able to write one down from memory.

Deep practice extensions

Use these after the main practice set. They are designed to force definition-level reasoning, theorem selection, and mixed application rather than routine calculation only.

Extension 1 · What is being approximated

State in one sentence what $Df(a)$ approximates.

Guide
The increment $f(a+h)-f(a)$, to within an error small compared with $\|h\|$.
Extension 2 · Why openness

Why must the domain be open in the definition?

Guide
The limit $h\to0$ must range over all directions. At a boundary point some directions leave the domain and the quotient is undefined there.
Extension 3 · Bilinear maps

What is the derivative of a bilinear map $B$ at $(a,b)$?

Guide
$(h,k)\mapsto B(a,k)+B(h,b)$. The leftover $B(h,k)$ is quadratic in $\|(h,k)\|$, hence $o(\|(h,k)\|)$. Practice 5 is the case $B(x,y)=xy$.
Extension 4 · Where the gradient points

Why is $\nabla f(a)$ perpendicular to the level set through $a$?

Guide
Along a curve inside the level set, $f$ is constant, so the chain rule gives $\nabla f(a)\cdot\gamma'(0)=0$. Every tangent direction to the level set arises this way.
