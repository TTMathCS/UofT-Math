Sequences

Sequences bring the ε–δ idea back, now as ε–N: a tail that stays arbitrarily close to a limit. They are the foundation for series in the final units.

By the end you can
  • State the ε–N definition of a sequence limit.
  • Use the limit laws and squeeze theorem for sequences.
  • Apply the Monotone Convergence Theorem.
  • Handle recursively defined sequences.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Sequences make "getting arbitrarily close" precise in the simplest setting, and they are the scaffolding for series. Cauchy sequences are even used to build the real numbers themselves.

In the real world

Every iterative algorithm is a sequence converging (you hope) to an answer — Newton's method, fixed-point iteration, and the training loops of machine learning all live here. The convergence rate is exactly how fast they finish.

Story & open questions

Zeno's paradox (~450 BC) — Achilles can never catch the tortoise — is really a question about the limit of a sequence, and the limit resolves it. Two millennia later, Cantor and Dedekind used Cauchy sequences to construct $\mathbb{R}$ rigorously. The ratio of consecutive Fibonacci numbers tends to the golden ratio, a limit that also appears in mathematical models of phyllotaxis.

The ε–N definition

Definition Sequence limit

$\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L$ means: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N\in\mathbb N$ such that $n\ge N\Rightarrow|a_n-L|<\varepsilon$.

Example

Prove $\lim\dfrac{n}{n+1}=1$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, $\left|\dfrac{n}{n+1}-1 \right|=\dfrac{1}{n+1}<\varepsilon$ once $n>\tfrac1\varepsilon-1$, so take $N=\lceil1/\varepsilon\rceil$.

Limit laws and squeeze

Tools

Limits of sums, products, and quotients behave as expected. Squeeze: if $b_n\le a_n\le c_n$ and $b_n,c_n\to L$, then $a_n\to L$.

Example

$\dfrac{\sin n}{n}\to0$ since $-\dfrac1n\le\dfrac{\sin n}{n}\le\dfrac1n$.

Monotone Convergence Theorem

Theorem MCT

A sequence that is increasing and bounded above converges (to its supremum). Likewise decreasing and bounded below.

Example A recursive sequence

$a_1=\sqrt2$, $a_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+a_n}$.

By induction $a_n<2$ (bounded) and $a_{n+1}>a_n$ (increasing), so by MCT it converges to some $L$.
Taking limits in the recursion, $L=\sqrt{2+L}$, so $L^2-L-2=0$, giving $L=2$.
Common pitfalls

• $N$ may depend on $\varepsilon$ but not on $n$.
• A bounded sequence need not converge (e.g. $(-1)^n$); monotonicity is what MCT adds.
• When solving $L=f(L)$, also confirm the limit exists first.

Practice

Problems adapted from the MAT137 question bank by Alfonso Gracia-Saz, used under CC BY-SA 4.0. Write full solutions before opening the answer.

Practice 1 · Definition

Prove $\lim\dfrac{2n+1}{n}=2$ from the definition.

Solution
$\left|\dfrac{2n+1}{n}-2\right|=\dfrac1n<\varepsilon$ for $n>1/\varepsilon$.
Practice 2 · Squeeze

Does $a_n=\dfrac{n!}{n^n}$ converge? To what?

Hint
$0<a_n\le\dfrac1n$ (each factor $\le1$, one equals $1/n$), so by squeeze $a_n\to0$.
Practice 3 · Recurrence

$a_1=1$, $a_{n+1}=\tfrac12(a_n+2/a_n)$. Show it converges and find the limit.

Answer
All terms are positive, and $a_2=\tfrac32>\sqrt2$. AM-GM gives $a_{n+1}\ge\sqrt2$, and whenever $a_n\ge\sqrt2$, $a_{n+1}\le a_n$. Thus the tail is decreasing and bounded below, so it converges. Its positive limit solves $L=\tfrac12(L+2/L)$, giving $L=\sqrt2$.
Practice 4 · Definition

Prove $\lim\dfrac{n^2}{n^2+1}=1$ directly from the definition.

Solution
$\left|\dfrac{n^2}{n^2+1}-1\right|=\dfrac{1}{n^2+1}<\dfrac{1}{n}<\varepsilon$ once $n>1/\varepsilon$.
Practice 5 · Growth rates

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n!+2e^n}{3n!+4e^n}$.

Answer
$n!$ dominates $e^n$; divide by $n!$ to get $\dfrac{1+0}{3+0}=\dfrac13.$
Practice 6 · Growth rates

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{5n^5+5^n+5\,n!}{n^n}$.

Answer
$n^n$ grows faster than $n!$, which beats $5^n$ and any power. Every term over $n^n\to0$, so the limit is $0$.
Practice 7 · Theory

Prove: if a sequence is increasing and unbounded above, it diverges to $\infty$.

Solution
Given $M$, unboundedness gives some $a_N>M$; since the sequence increases, $a_n\ge a_N>M$ for all $n\ge N$. That is the definition of $a_n\to\infty$.
Practice 8 · Recurrence

$R_0=1$, $R_{n+1}=\dfrac{R_n+2}{R_n+3}$. Show it converges and find the limit.

Solution
Let $L=\sqrt3-1$, the positive fixed point of $g(x)=\dfrac{x+2}{x+3}$. For $x>L$, one checks that $L<g(x)<x$. Since $R_0=1>L$, induction shows that $(R_n)$ decreases and remains above $L$, so it converges. Taking limits in the recurrence gives $R_n\to L=\sqrt3-1$.
