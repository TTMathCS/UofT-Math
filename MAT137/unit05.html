The Mean Value Theorem & Applications

The MVT is the workhorse that turns information about $f'$ into information about $f$. Almost every "derivative tells you about the function" fact — monotonicity, constancy, inequalities — comes from it.

By the end you can
  • State and prove Rolle's theorem and the Mean Value Theorem.
  • Prove that $f'=0$ on an interval forces $f$ constant.
  • Relate the sign of $f'$ to increasing/decreasing behaviour.
  • Prove inequalities with the MVT.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

The Mean Value Theorem is the bridge from a derivative at one instant to behaviour over a whole stretch. It is why "if your speed never topped 100, your average can't have either" is a theorem, not just a feeling.

In the real world

Average-speed cameras on highways literally issue tickets using the MVT — the time between two points gives an average speed the law treats as proof you sped somewhere. The same theorem underlies error bounds in numerical methods and uniqueness proofs for differential equations.

Story & open questions

It is named partly for Rolle (1691), who was himself an early skeptic of calculus. Cauchy gave it its modern form. Simple as it looks, almost every "the derivative controls the function" fact in this course flows from this one theorem.

What this unit changes

Before the Mean Value Theorem, a derivative is local information: it describes behavior at one point. The MVT turns that local information into a statement about an entire interval. That is why it becomes the proof engine for monotonicity, inequalities, uniqueness, and "same derivative means same function up to a constant."

MVT proof pattern
Choose two points $a<b$ in the interval.
Check $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$.
Use $f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)$ for some $c\in(a,b)$.
Insert information about $f'(c)$ to conclude something about $f(b)-f(a)$.

Typical proof goals

Many MVT problems look different but share the same skeleton. Learn to identify the target before doing computations.

  • Show increasing. Prove $f(x_2)-f(x_1)>0$ for arbitrary $x_1<x_2$.
  • Show constant. Prove $f(x_2)-f(x_1)=0$ for arbitrary $x_1<x_2$.
  • Prove an inequality. Apply MVT to the difference between the two sides, or bound $f'(c)$ directly.
  • Show uniqueness of a root. Combine IVT for existence with monotonicity or Rolle's theorem for at most one root.

Local extrema and Fermat's theorem

Theorem Fermat

If $f$ has a local extremum at an interior point $c$ and $f'(c)$ exists, then $f'(c)=0$.

Such points (where $f'=0$ or $f'$ fails to exist) are the critical points — the only candidates for interior extrema.

Rolle's theorem

Theorem Rolle

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and $f(a)=f(b)$, then there is $c\in(a,b)$ with $f'(c)=0$.

Proof idea
By the Extreme Value Theorem $f$ attains a max and min on $[a,b]$.
If the maximum equals the minimum, $f$ is constant and $f'=0$ everywhere. Otherwise, because $f(a)=f(b)$, at least one extremum is interior, and Fermat gives $f'(c)=0$. ∎

The Mean Value Theorem

Theorem MVT

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, then there is $c\in(a,b)$ with $$f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.$$

Proof idea
Subtract the secant line: let $g(x)=f(x)-\big[f(a)+ \tfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x-a)\big]$.
Then $g(a)=g(b)=0$; Rolle gives $g'(c)=0$, which rearranges to the MVT. ∎

Consequences

Corollaries

On an interval: if $f'=0$ everywhere then $f$ is constant; if $f'>0$ then $f$ is strictly increasing; if $f'=g'$ then $f-g$ is constant.

Example An inequality

Show $e^x\ge1+x$ for all $x$.

Let $h(x)=e^x-1-x$, so $h(0)=0$ and $h'(x)=e^x-1$.
$h'>0$ for $x>0$ and $h'<0$ for $x<0$, so $x=0$ is a global minimum; thus $h(x)\ge0$. ∎
Common pitfalls

• The MVT needs continuity on the closed interval and differentiability on the open one — check both.
• The MVT guarantees $c$ exists but not its value or uniqueness.
• "$f'>0$ at a point" does not make $f$ increasing near that point in general; the clean statement is about $f'>0$ on a whole interval.

Practice

Problems adapted from the MAT137 question bank by Alfonso Gracia-Saz, used under CC BY-SA 4.0. Write full solutions before opening the answer.

Practice 1 · Zero derivative

Use the MVT to prove: if $f'(x)=0$ for all $x\in(a,b)$, then $f$ is constant on $(a,b)$.

Solution
For any $x_1<x_2$ in the interval, MVT gives $f(x_2)-f(x_1)=f'(c)(x_2-x_1)=0$, so $f(x_2)=f(x_1)$.
Practice 2 · Lipschitz bound

Prove $|\sin a-\sin b|\le|a-b|$ for all real $a,b$.

Hint
Apply the MVT to $\sin$ on $[a,b]$: the difference equals $\cos c\,(a-b)$, and $|\cos c|\le1$.
Practice 3 · Inequality

Show $\ln(1+x)<x$ for all $x>0$.

Hint
Let $h(x)=x-\ln(1+x)$; $h(0)=0$ and $h'(x)=1-\tfrac1{1+x}>0$ for $x>0$, so $h$ increases.
Practice 4 · Bounding

If $f'(x)\le M$ on $[a,b]$, bound $f(b)-f(a)$.

Answer
MVT gives $f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)\le M(b-a)$.
Practice 5 · Exponential bound

Prove that $e^x\ge1+x$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$.

Solution
Let $g(x)=e^x-1-x$. Then $g(0)=0$ and $g'(x)=e^x-1$, which is $<0$ for $x<0$ and $>0$ for $x>0$. So $g$ has a global minimum $0$ at $x=0$; hence $g(x)\ge0$.
Practice 6 · Counting roots

Prove that a polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ real zeros.

Solution
If it had $n+1$ distinct zeros, Rolle's theorem between consecutive zeros gives $n$ zeros of the derivative, then $n-1$ of the second derivative, and so on, forcing the degree-$n$ leading term ($n!\,a_n\ne0$) to have a zero — impossible.
Practice 7 · One-to-one

Prove: if $f$ is differentiable on an open interval $I$ and $f'(x)\ne0$ for all $x\in I$, then $f$ is one-to-one on $I$.

Solution
If $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$ with $x_1<x_2$, Rolle's theorem gives $c\in(x_1,x_2)$ with $f'(c)=0$, contradicting the hypothesis. So $f$ is one-to-one.
Practice 8 · A derivative at 0

Suppose $f$ is differentiable at $0$ with $f(0)=0$ and $f(x)\ge x$ for all $x$. Find $f'(0)$.

Solution
For $x>0$, $\tfrac{f(x)}{x}\ge1$; for $x<0$, dividing by a negative flips it to $\tfrac{f(x)}{x}\le1$. The two one-sided limits are both $f'(0)$, so $f'(0)\ge1$ and $f'(0)\le1$, giving $f'(0)=1$.
