ENG140Y1: reading closely, arguing well.

Recent literature in English and how it answers the world it was written into. Across poetry, prose and drama the skill is the same: notice what the text is doing, work out why, and make a case a sceptical reader would accept.

About this course

The only full-year course in this first year.

ENG140Y1 "Literature for our Time" is an exploration of how recent literature in English responds to our world, including poetry, prose and drama by major writers of the twentieth century and emerging writers of the current century. It runs 48 lecture hours and 24 tutorial hours across both terms and carries 1.0 credit. It satisfies Creative and Cultural Representations (breadth category 1).

What this guide can and cannot give you. The set texts change every year with the instructor, and are not published anywhere in advance. So this guide teaches the method — how to read a poem, a novel and a play closely, the critical vocabulary that gets used, and how to build an argument in an essay. That transfers to whatever appears on the syllabus. For the reading list itself, check Quercus once the course opens.

02

Texts & Reading

The calendar specifies the kinds of text without naming titles, and the reading list is set by the instructor each year. What follows is what the calendar does establish.

What the calendar commits to
Three genres

"Poetry, prose, and drama" — all three appear, so expect the year to be divided among them. Units 2 to 6 follow that division.

Recent writing

"Major writers of the twentieth century and emerging writers of the current century." The period is modern and contemporary, not medieval or Victorian. Unit 7 covers what that implies about approach.

A guiding question

"How recent literature in English responds to our world." Expect the course to keep asking what a text is doing in its moment, not only what it says.

Tutorials matter

24 tutorial hours alongside 48 of lecture. Discussion sections in English courses normally carry participation marks and are where essays get workshopped.

ANNOTATE

Read with a pen

Mark what surprises you, what repeats, what jars. An essay grows out of the marks in the margin, and you cannot reconstruct them from a clean page.

TWICE

Read poems more than once

Once for sense, once aloud for sound, once for pattern. Poems are short enough that this costs minutes and changes what you see.

QUOTE

Work from the text

Every claim needs words on the page behind it. An essay that could have been written from a plot summary will be marked as one.

ARGUE

Take a position

A thesis someone could disagree with. Summary is not argument, and it is the single most common reason a first-year English essay loses marks.

03

Practice Plan

Literary skill is built by writing, not by reading about writing. The unit pages give exercises; use this course-level mix through the year.

01

One paragraph a week

Pick any passage and write a single close-reading paragraph. Claim, quotation, analysis. Twenty of these across the year is more practice than the essays alone provide.

02

Read the assignment twice

Once when set, once before drafting. Marks are lost to unanswered questions more often than to weak ideas.

03

Draft early, revise late

A first draft finished a week early and left alone reads very differently on return. Same effort, better essay.

04

Use office hours

Bring a thesis, not a finished essay. Ten minutes on whether an argument holds is worth more than any amount of polishing an argument that does not.

Course-wide challenge bank

These work on any text, which is the point — the method is what transfers.

Challenge 1 · Summary or argument

Which of these is a thesis? (a) "The poem is about grief." (b) "The poem's regular metre works against its subject, so its formal control enacts a grief the speaker will not state."

Solution sketch
(b). The first is a description nobody would dispute, so there is nothing to argue. The second makes a claim about how the poem works that a reader could reject — and can be supported or refuted from the text. Disputability is the test.
Challenge 2 · Evidence

You claim a narrator is unreliable. What would prove it?

Solution sketch
A gap between what the narrator says and what the text lets you see — a detail they report but misread, a contradiction between two accounts, a reaction from another character that does not fit their version. Quote the gap. Saying "the narrator seems unreliable" is not evidence.
Challenge 3 · Form and content

Why does it matter that a poem is a sonnet?

Solution sketch
Because the form carries expectations — fourteen lines, a turn, a resolving couplet or sestet — and the poem either meets them or breaks them. Both are meaningful. A sonnet that refuses its turn is saying something by refusing. Naming the form is only the first step; the argument is about what the poem does with it.
Challenge 4 · Context

When does historical context strengthen a reading, and when is it padding?

Solution sketch
It helps when it changes how a specific passage reads — a word that meant something different, a reference a first audience would have caught. It is padding when it is biography or general history bolted on with no consequence for the words. Test: does removing the context paragraph weaken the argument? If not, cut it.
