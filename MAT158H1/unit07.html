The Mean Value Theorem

The theorem that turns local information about $f'$ into global information about $f$. In analysis it is the lever for nearly every "derivative controls the function" result.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

It is the rigorous bridge from a derivative at one point to behaviour across a whole interval — why $f'=0$ everywhere forces $f$ constant, a fact we cannot prove without it.

In the real world

Average-speed cameras use it literally; numerical analysis uses it for error bounds; differential equations use it for uniqueness of solutions.

Story & open questions

Named partly for Rolle (1691), himself an early critic of calculus; Cauchy gave the modern statement and the generalized (Cauchy) Mean Value Theorem behind L'Hôpital's rule.

By the end you can
  • Prove Rolle's theorem and the MVT.
  • Deduce monotonicity and constancy from the sign of $f'$.
  • Prove inequalities with the MVT.

Rolle and the MVT

Theorem Rolle

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and $f(a)=f(b)$, then $f'(c)=0$ for some $c\in(a,b)$.

Theorem MVT

Under the same smoothness, $f'(c)=\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ for some $c\in(a,b)$.

Proof idea
Subtract the secant: $g(x)=f(x)-\big[f(a)+\tfrac{f(b)-f(a)} {b-a}(x-a)\big]$ has $g(a)=g(b)=0$.
Rolle gives $g'(c)=0$, which is the MVT. ∎

Consequences

Corollary

On an interval: $f'=0\Rightarrow f$ constant; $f'>0\Rightarrow f$ strictly increasing; $f'=g'\Rightarrow f-g$ constant.

Common pitfalls

• Need continuity on $[a,b]$ and differentiability on $(a,b)$ — check both.
• The MVT gives existence of $c$, not its value.
• "$f'(x_0)>0$" at one point does not make $f$ increasing near $x_0$.

Practice

Practice 1

Prove: $f'=0$ on an interval $\Rightarrow f$ constant there.

Solution
For any $x_1<x_2$, MVT gives $f(x_2)-f(x_1)=f'(c)(x_2-x_1)=0$.
Practice 2

Show $|\sin a-\sin b|\le|a-b|$.

Hint
MVT on $\sin$: $|\cos c|\,|a-b|\le|a-b|$.
Practice 3

Prove $e^x\ge1+x$ for all $x$.

Hint
$h(x)=e^x-1-x$ has $h(0)=0$ and $h'$ changes sign at $0$, a global min.
