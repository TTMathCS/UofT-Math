Inverse Functions

When does a function have a continuous, differentiable inverse — and what is its derivative? This one-variable theorem is the seed of one of the deepest results in all of analysis.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Many essential functions — $\exp$, $\arcsin$, $\arctan$ — are defined as inverses. We need to know they are differentiable and to compute their derivatives without an explicit formula.

In the real world

The multivariable inverse and implicit function theorems (built on this one) underlie change-of-variables in integration, the Jacobians of robotics, and the solvability conditions in optimization.

Story & open questions

The 1-D version is elementary, but its generalizations are cornerstones of MAT257 and of differential geometry — the language of general relativity.

By the end you can
  • Show a strictly monotone continuous function is invertible.
  • Prove the inverse is continuous.
  • Differentiate an inverse: $(f^{-1})'(y)=\dfrac{1}{f'(x)}$.

Existence and continuity of the inverse

Theorem

A continuous, strictly monotone function on an interval is a bijection onto its image, with a continuous, strictly monotone inverse.

Derivative of the inverse

Theorem

Let $f$ be one-to-one on an interval with continuous inverse. If $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, $f'(x_0)\ne0$, and $y_0=f(x_0)$, then $f^{-1}$ is differentiable at $y_0$ and $(f^{-1})'(y_0)=\dfrac{1}{f'(x_0)}$.

Example arcsin
$y=\arcsin x\Rightarrow\sin y=x\Rightarrow\cos y\,y'=1$.
$\cos y=\sqrt{1-x^2}$ on $[-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2]$, so $y'=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$ for $|x|<1$. At $x=\pm1$, arcsine is not differentiable as a real-valued function.
Common pitfalls

• The derivative formula needs $f'(x)\ne0$; at a horizontal tangent the inverse has a vertical one.
• Track the domain of the inverse and the correct sign of the square root.
• Continuity plus strict monotonicity on an interval gives the cleanest sufficient condition for a continuous inverse.

Practice

Practice 1

Show $\dfrac{d}{dx}\arctan x=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}$.

Solution
$\tan y=x\Rightarrow \sec^2y\,y'=1$, and $\sec^2y=1+x^2$.
Practice 2

If $f(x)=x^3+x$, find $(f^{-1})'(2)$.

Hint
$f(1)=2$, $f'(1)=4$, so $(f^{-1})'(2)=\tfrac14$.
Practice 3

Why is $f(x)=x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$ not invertible, but on $[0,\infty)$ it is?

Answer
Not monotone on $\mathbb{R}$ (fails injectivity); strictly increasing on $[0,\infty)$.
