Eigenvalues & Diagonalization

The finale: directions the map merely stretches. Find enough of them and the operator becomes diagonal — the simplest matrix it can ever have. Everything in the course converges here: determinants find the eigenvalues, null spaces find the eigenvectors, and change of basis cashes them in.

By the end you can
  • Compute eigenvalues via the characteristic polynomial and eigenvectors via null spaces.
  • Prove eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent.
  • Decide diagonalizability and carry it out.
  • State Cayley–Hamilton and verify it in examples.
Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Diagonalizing decouples a linear system into independent one-dimensional ones. Powers, exponentials, and long-run behaviour of a matrix all become trivial in an eigenbasis.

In the real world

Google's PageRank is an eigenvector; principal component analysis diagonalizes a covariance matrix; vibration modes, quantum energy levels, and Markov chain limits are all eigenvalue problems.

Story & open questions

"Eigen" is Hilbert's German for "own/characteristic." Cayley stated the Cayley–Hamilton theorem in 1858 after checking it on small matrices. When diagonalization fails, the Jordan canonical form (MAT247) finishes the classification.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Definition

A nonzero $v$ is an eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$ if $Av=\lambda v$. The eigenspace is $E_\lambda=N(A-\lambda I)$ — a subspace, consisting of all eigenvectors for $\lambda$ together with $0$.

Theorem How to find them

$\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ iff $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$: an eigenvector is a nonzero vector in $N(A-\lambda I)$, which exists exactly when $A-\lambda I$ is singular. So the eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic polynomial $f(t)=\det(A-tI)$, and each eigenspace is computed by row reduction (Unit 7).

Example Full run

$A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

$f(t)=\det\!\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2-t&1\\0&3-t \end{smallmatrix}\right)=(2-t)(3-t)$: eigenvalues $2$ and $3$.
$E_2=N\!\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix} \right)=\operatorname{span}\{(1,0)\}$; $E_3=N\!\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-1&1\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right) =\operatorname{span}\{(1,1)\}$ — check $A(1,1)=(3,3)$. ✓
Two independent eigenvectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$: with $P=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, $P^{-1}AP=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

Independence and the diagonalizability test

Theorem Distinct eigenvalues

Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent. Hence an $n\times n$ matrix with $n$ distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable.

Proof (two eigenvalues; induction handles more)
Suppose $av_1+bv_2=0$ with $Av_i=\lambda_iv_i$, $\lambda_1\ne\lambda_2$. Apply $A$: $a\lambda_1v_1+b\lambda_2v_2=0$.
Subtract $\lambda_1$ times the first equation: $b(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)v_2=0$. Since $\lambda_2\ne\lambda_1$ and $v_2\ne0$, $b=0$; then $av_1=0$ gives $a=0$. ∎
Theorem Diagonalizability

$A$ is diagonalizable iff there is a basis of eigenvectors — equivalently, the eigenspace dimensions add up to $n$. Each eigenspace dimension (geometric multiplicity) is at least $1$ and at most the root's multiplicity in $f(t)$ (algebraic multiplicity); diagonalizable means every eigenvalue reaches that ceiling and $f$ splits over the field. Then $P$ (eigenvectors as columns) gives $P^{-1}AP=D$.

Example Two failure modes
Defective: $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ has only the eigenvalue $1$ with algebraic multiplicity $2$, but $E_1=N\!\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ is only one-dimensional — no second independent eigenvector, not diagonalizable.
Wrong field: the rotation $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ has $f(t)=t^2+1$ with no real roots — no real eigenvalues at all. Over $\mathbb{C}$ it has eigenvalues $\pm i$ and diagonalizes. Unit 9's field-dependence, live.
Why diagonal is the jackpot If $A=PDP^{-1}$ then $A^k=PD^kP^{-1}$, and powering a diagonal matrix means powering numbers. Linear recurrences, Markov chains, and systems of ODEs are solved by exactly this move.

Cayley–Hamilton

Theorem Cayley–Hamilton

Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial: $f(A)=0$. Consequently the minimal polynomial (Unit 9) divides the characteristic polynomial.

Example Verify it

For $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, $f(t)=t^2-5t+6$.

$A^2=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&5\\0&9\end{smallmatrix} \right)$, so $A^2-5A+6I=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4-10+6&5-5\\0&9-15+6\end{smallmatrix} \right)=0$. ✓
Warning: "substitute $A$ into $\det(A-tI)$" is not a proof — $\det(A-AI)=\det 0=0$ is a scalar statement, not the matrix identity $f(A)=0$. The real proof is one of the highlights of the course.
Common pitfalls

• Eigenvectors are nonzero by definition; $E_\lambda$ contains $0$ but $0$ is never an eigenvector.
• Distinct eigenvalues guarantee diagonalizability, but repeated ones do not forbid it ($I$ has one eigenvalue and is already diagonal).
• Check geometric multiplicities — a repeated root with a thin eigenspace is exactly how diagonalization fails.
• Eigenvalues live in the field: real matrices can have complex eigenvalues, and "diagonalizable" must name the field.

Practice

Original problems written for this guide in the style of Friedberg, Insel & Spence exercises. Write a full solution before opening the answer.

Practice 1 · Full run

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

Answer
$\lambda=2$ with $(1,0)$; $\lambda=3$ with $(1,1)$. Diagonalizable, since the two are independent.
Practice 2 · Defective

Is $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ diagonalizable?

Answer
No: the only eigenvalue is $1$ and its eigenspace is $\operatorname{span}\{(1,0)\}$, dimension $1<2$.
Practice 3 · Symmetric swap

Diagonalize $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

Solution
$f(t)=t^2-1$: eigenvalues $1$ and $-1$, eigenvectors $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$. With those as the columns of $P$, $P^{-1}AP=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
Practice 4 · Independence

Prove: eigenvectors for two distinct eigenvalues are independent.

Solution
From $av_1+bv_2=0$, apply $A$ and subtract $\lambda_1$ times the original: $b(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)v_2=0$, so $b=0$, then $a=0$.
Practice 5 · Field matters

Show the rotation $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ has no real eigenvalues but two complex ones.

Solution
$f(t)=t^2+1$ has no real roots; over $\mathbb{C}$ the eigenvalues are $i$ and $-i$ (with eigenvectors $(1,-i)$ and $(1,i)$).
Practice 6 · Cayley–Hamilton

Verify Cayley–Hamilton for $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

Solution
$f(t)=t^2-5t+6$ and $A^2=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&5\\0&9\end{smallmatrix}\right)$; compute $A^2-5A+6I=0$ entry by entry.
Practice 7 · Triangular

Prove the eigenvalues of a triangular matrix are its diagonal entries.

Solution
$A-tI$ is triangular, so $\det(A-tI)=\prod_i(a_{ii}-t)$ (Unit 8) — the roots are exactly the diagonal entries.
Practice 8 · Projections again

Suppose $A=PDP^{-1}$ where $D$ is diagonal with only $0$s and $1$s on the diagonal. Prove $A^2=A$.

Solution
$D^2=D$ since $0^2=0$ and $1^2=1$; then $A^2=PD^2P^{-1}=PDP^{-1}=A$ — diagonalization turns matrix identities into scalar ones.
