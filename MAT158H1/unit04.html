Continuous Functions

Continuity is "no sudden jumps," made precise with ε–δ. It is the hypothesis that powers the three hard theorems of the next unit.

Why it matters & where it comes from
Why it exists

Almost every useful theorem about functions assumes continuity. Pinning down exactly what it means is what lets those theorems be proved rather than believed.

In the real world

Continuity is the "small change in input gives small change in output" assumption behind stable control systems, well-posed physics models, and convergent numerical methods.

Story & open questions

Bolzano and Cauchy gave the modern definition in the early 1800s. Pathologies soon followed — functions continuous only at a single point, or nowhere differentiable — showing how much the precise definition was needed.

By the end you can
  • State continuity at a point in ε–δ form.
  • Prove that sums, products, and compositions stay continuous.
  • Decide continuity of a patched function.

Definition

Definition Continuity

$f$ is continuous at $a$ if $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$; equivalently, $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0$ with $|x-a|<\delta\Rightarrow |f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon$.

Building continuous functions

Theorem

Sums, products, quotients (nonzero denominator), and compositions of continuous functions are continuous. Polynomials are continuous everywhere.

Example Composition

If $g$ is continuous at $a$ and $f$ at $g(a)$, then $f\circ g$ is continuous at $a$ — chase the ε through $f$, then through $g$.

Patched functions

Example

$f(x)=x\sin(1/x)$ for $x\ne0$, $f(0)=0$. Since $|f(x)|\le|x|\to0$, $\lim_{x\to0}f=0=f(0)$, so $f$ is continuous at $0$.

Common pitfalls

• Continuity needs the value $f(a)$ to exist and equal the limit.
• A removable discontinuity (limit exists $\ne f(a)$) is still a discontinuity.
• Continuity of $f\circ g$ needs $f$ continuous at $g(a)$, not at $a$.

Practice

Practice 1

Where is $f(x)=\dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ (with $f(1)=2$) continuous?

Answer
Everywhere: for $x\ne1$ it equals $x+1$, and the limit at $1$ is $2=f(1)$.
Practice 2

Prove the sum of two functions continuous at $a$ is continuous at $a$.

Hint
Apply the sum law for limits; the limit equals $f(a)+g(a)$.
Practice 3

Is the Dirichlet function (1 on rationals, 0 on irrationals) continuous anywhere?

Answer
Nowhere — every interval contains both rationals and irrationals, so no limit exists.
